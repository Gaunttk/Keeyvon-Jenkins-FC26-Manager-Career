Second Place After Seventeen Matches: Why Wrexham's First Premier League Campaign Already Belongs in the History Books

Whatever happens between now and May, what Keeyvon Jenkins has built in barely eighteen months at the Racecourse has already crossed a threshold English football rarely sees from a newly promoted club.

Oliver Hargreaves
Senior Football Correspondent · Sky Sports · Dec 20, 2026

There is a version of this column that gets written every few years, usually in spring, usually after the fact: a newly promoted club held the line long enough to make the rest of the division briefly nervous, and by the time the final table was printed, the romance had curdled into relief that normal service had resumed. Seventeen matches into their first season in the Premier League, Wrexham are threatening to make me write a different column entirely — and for once, the case does not need much dressing up.

Second in the table. Thirty-six points from seventeen games. A goal difference of plus-23, the best of any side in the division, built on the best defensive record in the league — ten goals conceded, nobody else within seven of them. A run to the Carabao Cup semi-finals that included, three days after a ninetieth-minute league defeat to the same opponent, taking an academy-heavy XI to the Etihad and beating a Manchester City side fielding real first-team players. And underneath all of it, a Championship title from the season before that nobody outside Wrexham seriously expects to be repeated by most clubs who go up in their place.

Keeyvon Jenkins did not inherit this. He was appointed in January of last year to a club still finding its feet in the Championship, and what he has built since — a defensive record most established top-flight sides would frame on the wall, an academy pipeline producing first-team minutes rather than loan-list fodder, a squad assembled with the kind of coherence that usually takes seasons to establish — did not happen by accident. It is worth being precise about what that comparison actually requires, because English football has only a handful of promoted-club campaigns that earn the historical company Wrexham's form is now inviting.

Brian Clough's Nottingham Forest is the obvious starting point, and the obvious caveat: Forest were promoted in 1977, finished third in their first season back in the old First Division, then won it outright in their second, and followed that with back-to-back European Cups. Nobody sane is promising Wrexham a Clough trajectory — that run remains one of the genuine impossibilities of the English game, a fluke of timing and managerial genius unlikely to be repeated. But the underlying mechanism is recognisable: an unfashionable club, a manager building a specific identity rather than buying one off the shelf, and results that arrive not despite the modesty of the operation but because of the coherence of it. Alf Ramsey's Ipswich Town offers the tighter parallel — promoted in 1961, and remarkably, champions of the entire top flight the very next season, on a tactical setup so far ahead of what the rest of the division had scouted for that opponents spent half the campaign working out what they were even facing. Wrexham's identity under Jenkins is not a secret in the same way Ramsey's wing-less formation was in 1962. But the underlying story — a provincial club punching miles above its transfer budget through tactical coherence and defensive organisation rather than star power — is the same story, told sixty-four years later.

The comparison every reader will reach for regardless is Leicester City's 2015-16 title, and it is worth saying plainly why that one fits less neatly than the other two: Leicester's miracle arrived in their second full season back in the top flight, not their first, and was built on a run of late-season form as much as a season-long defensive identity. Wrexham, seventeen games in, are further ahead of schedule than Leicester were at the same stage of their own story. That is not a prediction. It is an observation, and the two things are not the same.

Whatever happens between now and May — and second place after seventeen games guarantees nothing, least of all a title race against opposition with City's and Arsenal's resources — Wrexham have already produced one of the outstanding opening Premier League campaigns any promoted club has managed in the competition's history.

— Oliver Hargreaves, Sky Sports

None of this requires Wrexham to actually win the league to matter. English football's history is not short of promoted sides who flattered in August and September before the division's superior resources ground them back down by Easter; it is much shorter on sides still holding the best defensive record in the country after seventeen matches, with a run to a domestic cup semi-final and a title in the division below achieved in the same eighteen-month window under the same manager. If Wrexham finish this season second, or fifth, or somewhere the algorithms would call a regression to the mean, that will not erase what these seventeen matches have already put on the record. Some seasons are judged entirely by where they end. This one has already justified itself by where it started.
