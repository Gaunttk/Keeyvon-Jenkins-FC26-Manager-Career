Keeyvon Jenkins Just Became Two Different American Soccer Stories at Once

The USWNT job lands on a résumé that already had FC Dallas, USMNT caps, a career-ending injury, and a Premier League title on it. Tara Bennett on why this one is harder to explain back home than any of the others.

Tara Bennett
U.S. Football Correspondent · ESPN FC · Sep 29, 2027

I've spent two seasons writing some version of the sentence "Keeyvon Jenkins is doing something no American has done before," and I genuinely thought I'd run out of new ways to say it after the Premier League title back in May. I was wrong. He just agreed to coach the U.S. Women's National Team while remaining in charge of a club playing in the Premier League, the Champions League, and two domestic cups simultaneously. There is no comparable dual mandate in the modern game that I can find, men's or women's. This isn't a testimonial appointment or an ambassadorial title. It's a full head coaching job, with a 12-month clause attached that will end it if he doesn't win a trophy.

Here's the part that's going to be hard to explain to a casual U.S. audience: Jenkins has never coached a women's team, at any level, for a single day. His entire playing and coaching résumé — FC Dallas academy, Fulham, Swansea, thirty-four USMNT caps as an outfield player, then the long way back through ESPN's own studio to Colorado, San Jose, Denmark, and finally Wrexham — is built entirely inside the men's game. That's not automatically disqualifying; plenty of good club structures translate across the gap. But it is exactly the kind of thing that gets asked in every single press conference for the next year, and Jenkins is going to have to have a real answer for it, not a deflection.

And yet. The reaction I've gotten from the handful of people I've called inside U.S. Soccer today isn't panic, it's curiosity bordering on relief — the federation has cycled through enough conventional hires in the women's program's recent history that a genuinely unconventional swing reads, to some of them, as a sign somebody upstairs is finally willing to bet on process over pedigree. That's the same bet Wrexham's ownership made on Jenkins back in January 2025, and it paid off in a way that still doesn't fully make sense on paper. Whether it pays off twice, in a completely different sport's power structure, on a completely different continent's calendar, with a completely different fanbase watching, is the actual story here — not the fact of the appointment, but the size of the swing.

I said yes because I think I can help. If that turns out to be wrong, I'd rather find out by trying than by never picking up the phone.

— Keeyvon Jenkins, in a brief statement following the announcement

Jenkins didn't lean on the flag or the symbolism in his own short statement today, which tracks with how he handled the Premier League title — his instinct is always to point at the work rather than the narrative around it. But the narrative exists whether he wants it or not, and for a U.S. audience it's going to sit right on top of every result for the next twelve months: an American who already won English football's biggest domestic prize the hard way is now betting that same process on a completely different team, in a completely different sport's spotlight, with a very public clock attached. Whatever happens with Wrexham's season from here, this is now happening in parallel with it, and both stories are being written at the same time.
