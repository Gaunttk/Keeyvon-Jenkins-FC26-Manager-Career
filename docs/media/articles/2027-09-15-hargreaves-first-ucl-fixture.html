Fifteen Years in the Wilderness, Then the Champions League Anthem. There Is No Modern Comparison Left to Reach For.

I wrote in May that Wrexham's Premier League title had no real precedent in the modern English game. I did not expect to be writing a follow-up to that column about European football four months later. There genuinely isn't a template for this.

Oliver Hargreaves
Senior Football Correspondent · Sky Sports · Sep 15, 2027

I have covered every Champions League final since 2003. I have sat in press boxes for the big domestic coronations — Leicester at the King Power, Ipswich's old Second Division title in the days before this competition even carried its current name — and I thought, after May, that I had used up my supply of “there is no comparable case” columns on this particular football club for at least a season. Wrexham have made a liar of that plan in four months. On Tuesday night, at a stadium in continental Europe that most of their own supporters would have struggled to locate on a map two years ago, Wrexham AFC played a UEFA Champions League match. That sentence still doesn't sit naturally next to this club's name, and it is worth pausing on exactly why.

Start with the shape of the last two decades. Relegated out of the Football League entirely in 2008, Wrexham spent fifteen seasons in the National League — English football's fifth tier, the level clubs go to disappear rather than to build launchpads. They won that division in 2022-23 with a record 111 points, and then did something no club had done before them: three straight promotions across the top five divisions of the English pyramid, League Two to League One to the Championship, arriving in the second tier for the 2025-26 season. They won that too — 112 points, the Championship title at the first attempt. Promoted into the Premier League for 2026-27, they won that as well, a maiden top-flight title in their first-ever season in it, with an FA Cup added for good measure. Twelve months after a club was playing fifth-tier football against part-time opposition, it would have needed only one more trophy to complete a clean sweep of everything English football offers domestically. Now, in year three of the Premier League, it has stepped onto the one stage that sweep didn't include.

Nineteen years, door to door, from the fifth tier of English football to a Champions League away fixture against a genuine European institution. I don't say that to diminish what happened on the pitch on Tuesday, which was in its own right a perfectly respectable result — a scoreless draw, with Keeyvon Jenkins choosing, tellingly, to protect his strongest players for the weekend and hand the biggest night in the club's history to a team built largely around its own academy. “We're not here to make up the numbers,” Jenkins told me when I asked him about the draw in Manchester City's build-up this week. “We're here because we earned the right to be, and I'd rather find out what my kids are made of on a night like that than protect a result nobody expected us to get anyway.” It's a quietly confident thing for a manager to say about a Champions League trip, and on this occasion the result backed him up.

What Tuesday actually settles, in the end, is smaller than the trophy conversations of May but in some ways more significant: this club is no longer a promotion story with a ceiling. The question that used to attach itself to every stage of Wrexham's rise — could they survive at the level above? — has now been asked and answered four divisions in a row. There is no fifth division left to ask it about. What's left is only the competition English football's biggest clubs actually organise their entire calendars around, and Wrexham are in it. Not as a footnote fixture, not as a play-off qualifier making up the numbers in a lesser tournament, but in the competition itself, on merit, nineteen years after this exact same football club couldn't get out of the National League.

I have spent this week trying, again, to find the comparable case — the club that fell to the bottom and climbed all the way to a Champions League fixture inside two decades, with three domestic titles picked up along the way. I haven't found one. I said the same thing about the Premier League title in May and meant it then too. The honest answer, this time, might simply be that there isn't a version of this story that has happened before, and Wrexham are going to keep writing new chapters of it whether the record books have a category ready for them or not.
