Champions With a Minus Sign: The Numbers Behind Wrexham's Uneven Title Defense

Five Premier League games into defending a maiden top-flight title, Wrexham sit seventh with a negative goal difference. The record has more wins in it than the title-winning start did twelve months ago — but the process numbers, tracked in this kind of depth for the first time this season, tell a shakier story underneath it.

James McAllister
Tactical Analyst · The Athletic · Sep 19, 2027

Twelve months ago this column had no possession numbers, no shot counts, no pass-accuracy figures to work with — just results, and the results were extraordinary. Wrexham won the Premier League in their maiden top-flight season, 84 points from 38 games, a goal difference of plus-55. That team is still, on paper, the champion of England. The team that has taken the field five times in this Premier League season sits seventh, four points off the top, with a goal difference of minus-one. Both of those sentences are true at once, and the interesting question is what actually changed between them — because it isn't simply that Wrexham have started losing.

Go back to the equivalent five fixtures of the title-winning campaign and the comparison is not the collapse the table position implies. Last season's champions opened with two wins, two draws and a loss — Wolves and Bournemouth beaten, Sheffield United and Chelsea drawn, Tottenham the only defeat — for a goal difference of plus-two. This season's team has actually won a fixture more: three wins, no draws, two losses, for a goal difference of minus-one. More wins, worse goal difference, and a league table that is far more unforgiving about the difference than it was a year ago, because seven other clubs have also taken nine or more points from their first five games. The division has not gotten worse around a tiring champion. If anything, it has gotten better, and that alone accounts for a chunk of the seventh-place finish this data describes.

What is genuinely new this season is the underlying data itself — team-level possession, shot counts and pass accuracy were not tracked with any consistency during the title run, so there is no clean year-on-year process comparison to make. What the numbers that do exist show, though, is a team playing recognizably the same way it always has, just doing it against sterner opposition and, in two matches so far, getting badly outshot for it. Across the five league games, Wrexham have averaged 42.8% possession, 86.8% pass accuracy and 7.6 tackles a game — comfortably the identity that won the title, built on sitting deep and accepting the ball elsewhere. The shot counts are where it turns uncomfortable: 30 shots for against 35 against across the matches with full data, and in the two games where shots on target were logged — the win over Brentford and the defeat to Liverpool — the split was 3 to 18. That is one shot on target kept out for every six the opponent has managed, in the only sample this column has to check it against. The Community Shield defeat to Manchester City in August fits the same pattern — 41% possession, four shots to twelve, two on target to eight — and so, less alarmingly given the opposition and unfamiliar competition, does the goalless draw in Wrexham's first-ever Champions League match at Inter Milan.

A team can absorb 35 shots to 30 and still be fine, if the ones that matter are kept off target. Wrexham conceded eighteen shots on target in two Premier League matches and let in four goals from them. That ratio, not the possession number, is the stat to recheck in a month.

The other place this season looks nothing like last is the transfer market, and the two stories are related. The rebuild that actually produced the title winners was a wholesale one: thirteen players in and twenty-three out last summer, a combined outlay north of $85m across eight disclosed fees, with Rio Ngumoha ($22.5m), Leo Sauer ($22.1m) and Ayden Heaven ($18.4m) alone accounting for more than $60m of it. This summer, defending a title and entering a Champions League campaign for the first time, Jenkins spent $37.5m on one player — Lewis Miley, a club-record fee from Newcastle — added a free-transfer squad piece in Musa Tine, and sent eleven fringe or academy-aged players out on loan rather than selling them. That is not a club standing still; it is a club that spent last summer building a Premier League squad from close to scratch and this summer decided the squad it built didn't need replacing, just developing further out on loan. Miley's early return — a 7.4 rating on debut against Sunderland — is the kind of single data point this column flagged as too small to lean on before, and that caution still applies with one appearance on the board.

What that quieter window does put more weight on is the academy pipeline, and today's business is a useful marker for that: Knut Inge Bakke and Even Jensen, both eighteen, promoted from the Youth Academy into senior squad numbers, with Welsh centre-back Harry Bound, seventeen, brought in behind them. Neither Bakke nor Jensen is a finished Premier League player — 66 and 64 overall respectively, both a long way from their potential ranges — but a club that spent one-third of last summer's outlay on transfers this summer is, by necessity, leaning harder on exactly this conveyor belt than it did twelve months ago. The tactical identity hasn't moved. The squad-building strategy behind it has, from wholesale reconstruction to something closer to maintenance, and five games of harder opposition and a slimmer transfer window is not enough evidence to say that's the wrong call — only enough to say it's the thing actually worth watching, more than the possession count, as this data set grows through the autumn.
