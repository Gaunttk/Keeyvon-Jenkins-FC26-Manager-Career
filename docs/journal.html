Al Dawsari Ends Wrexham's Unbeaten Run as the New Year Begins With a Thud at the Amex

Three wins and a Carabao Cup shock of Manchester City had carried Wrexham into January full of momentum. A single second-half strike on the south coast was enough to stop it dead.

There is a particular flatness to a New Year's away trip on the south coast, squad legs still heavy from three matches in eleven days over Christmas, and Wrexham never quite shook it off at the Amex. Brighton, who had lost 1–0 to these same opponents back in September, set up cagier this time — deeper block, quicker to put bodies behind the ball whenever Amrizi got a yard of space — and for an hour it worked well enough to produce very little at either end. "We knew they'd set up different to the reverse fixture," Jenkins said afterward. "We didn't have an answer for it quickly enough, and against a team with nothing to lose that's usually when you get hurt."

They got hurt two minutes into the second half. Al Dawsari picked up a loose ball on the edge of the box after a half-cleared corner and curled it past Okonkwo before the visiting back line had reset — the kind of goal that punishes a slow restart rather than any sustained pressure. Wrexham pushed for an equalizer that never really threatened to arrive; Toni Fruk's half-chance from the edge of the box on the hour was the closest thing to a clear sight of goal all afternoon. "We didn't create enough to say we deserved something out of it," Fruk admitted in the mixed zone. "Some days the performance isn't there and you have to be honest about that instead of talking about bad luck."

It is the first defeat since Manchester City won 2–1 at the Racecourse on December 12th — a run that in between included a genuine statement result, beating a full-strength City side 2–0 in the Carabao Cup quarter-final with a youth-heavy XI just three days after that league loss. One of the few positives from an otherwise flat afternoon at the Amex was another look at Thiago Pitarch, signed from Real Madrid on transfer deadline eve, who came off the bench for his Wrexham debut and looked composed enough in a difficult half-hour to justify the fee nobody at the club will disclose. "He's not going to fix today on his own, but you could see the game slow down a little when he had it," Jenkins said of the midfielder. "That's the kind of calm we need more of when things aren't flowing."

We didn't create enough to say we deserved something out of it. Some days the performance isn't there and you have to be honest about that instead of talking about bad luck.

— Toni Fruk, post-match

The defeat drops Wrexham to third on goal difference, still very much inside the Champions League places with eighteen games to play, and a nine-day break before Chelsea visit the Racecourse gives Jenkins time to get the squad's legs back before the run resumes in earnest.

Three Weeks I'd Take Again, and a Flight Home I Wouldn't

From the low of losing to City to the Carabao Cup upset of the same club three days later, three straight league wins, and now a flat one to close it out — the whole thing in one stretch.

Three weeks ago I wrote in here about the gap between us and City being smaller than people think but still real, and closing it being the whole job. Three days after I wrote that, we went to the Etihad with a team full of kids and beat their first XI 2–0 in the cup. I'm not going to pretend that result solved anything permanent — different competition, different stakes, different version of them on the touchline — but it told this group something true about itself that a league table can't: we're not scared of anybody in this country right now. We backed it up with three straight league wins over Brentford, West Ham, and Ipswich, and for about six days over Christmas we topped the Premier League table. I let myself feel that one properly this time.

And then today happened, and it's a useful correction to how good I was feeling. Brighton set up smart, we didn't have an answer quickly enough, and Dawsari punished the one mistake he needed. No City-level individual quality to point to this time — just a flat performance from a squad that's played a lot of football in a short window, on the road, in January, on the back of a result that maybe let a little air out of everyone without us noticing. Fruk said it best in the mixed zone: the performance wasn't there, and pretending otherwise doesn't help anybody. Pitarch's half-hour was the one thing I'll actually rewatch this week — calm on the ball in a game that badly needed someone calm on the ball.

We're not scared of anybody in this country right now. That's the real thing I take from the last three weeks — not the table position, which will move around plenty more before May.

— KJ

Amrizi and Sauer See Off Ipswich as a Teenager Named Soria Gets His First Taste

Wrexham survived a scare at Portman Road to stay in the Champions League places, while forty minutes down the touchline a home-grown eighteen-year-old made a first-team debut that cost the club nothing but four years of patience.

Portman Road on a wet Tuesday between Christmas and New Year is not where the Premier League's glamour lives, and for long stretches this did not look like a top-two fixture. Ipswich, bottom three and fighting for their lives, set up to make Wrexham as uncomfortable as a struggling side knows how — deep lines, bodies behind the ball, and a crowd that had waited all year for a Boxing Day-week banana skin. It nearly worked. "That's the hardest place we've played all season, and I include the away trips to City and Anfield in that," Jenkins admitted afterward. "When a team is fighting to stay up, they find something extra. We knew that going in."

Yacel Amrizi did what Yacel Amrizi does. Five minutes in, he turned a half-cleared corner into the opener, arriving at the back post ahead of two defenders who were still watching the ball in flight. Ipswich responded almost immediately — Davis Keillor-Dunn, once of this parish and still fondly remembered at the Racecourse for entirely different reasons, drilled a leveller past Arthur Okonkwo in the 12th minute that had the home end believing. The scrap that followed was scruffy, physical, and short on real chances until Leo Sauer, cutting inside from the left in the 65th minute, found the top corner from range to settle it. "We scraped it," Amrizi said flatly in the mixed zone, waving off a question about his goal involvement. "Away form in this league is decided by moments, not performances. We took ours, just about."

"I didn't take a phone call from anyone congratulating us for being top on the 25th, and I won't be taking one for being second on the 29th," he said. "Ask me in May."

The detail that will matter more in a year's time happened in the 66th minute, when Marco Soria — eighteen years old, signed into the academy as a raw, injury-interrupted prospect out of Córdoba back in March — came off the bench for his senior debut and promptly looked like he belonged, rating 6.8 in an hour that included one driven run down the left that had the Ipswich full-back reaching for a card he never got shown. "But days like today are the ones that tell you the academy is actually working, not just producing numbers on a spreadsheet. Marco earned that cap. Nobody gave it to him." Soria himself, still catching his breath pitchside, kept it simpler: "I've been dreaming about that since I was eleven years old, watching my dad's bus go past the training ground. I'm not going to pretend it wasn't emotional."

A Christmas Day Scare, Then Amrizi and Obi Send Wrexham Top of the Premier League

Two days after Oliver Hargreaves compared this campaign to Ipswich '62 and Forest '78, Wrexham went and did something neither of those sides could claim at Christmas — top spot, in their first-ever top-flight season.

There is no easing into a Christmas Day fixture, and West Ham made sure Wrexham found that out the hard way. Nine minutes in, Callum Doyle and Rio Ngumoha were both caught square as Castellanos ran off the last defender's shoulder and finished low past Bernt Klaverboer, who was making just his sixth appearance of the season in place of the rested Arthur Okonkwo. For a ground that had spent the morning singing carols outside the turnstiles, the Racecourse went quiet fast. "You could feel the crowd deflate a bit," Owen Meredith wrote from the press box. "Nobody wants to be chasing a game on the 25th of December."

They did not stay behind for long. Yacel Amrizi, whose habit of scoring at exactly the moment Wrexham need him to has become the defining trend of this Premier League campaign, levelled things in the 54th minute with the kind of low, first-time finish that has become his signature. Toni Fruk did the unglamorous work to make it happen, and Andrés Gómez — introduced from the bench in the second half — turned provider again for Chido Obi's 80th-minute winner, the teenager's ninth league goal of the season and another reminder of why Jenkins keeps finding minutes for him even with Fruk and Amrizi both firing. "Chido doesn't care what day it is or who's watching," Jenkins said afterward. "He just wants the ball in the box. That's a gift you can't coach into a player."

The man-of-the-match award, though, belonged to someone who had not featured at all a fortnight ago. Brian Gutiérrez, sidelined with a shoulder knock picked up in the closing stages at Brentford, was passed fit in time to start and produced arguably his best ninety minutes in a Wrexham shirt — a 7.8 rating built on the kind of composed midfield work that let Mokio and Bobadilla push forward without exposing the back line a second time. "Four days off with a shoulder problem and I come back and play my best game of the season," Gutiérrez said, laughing, after the final whistle. "Football doesn't always make sense." Jenkins was less surprised: "Brian's a player who trusts his prep. He came back into a group that had already shown it could win the ugly ones without him — he just made sure this one wasn't ugly for long."

The bigger story sits above the scoreline. Two days earlier, Sky Sports' Oliver Hargreaves had written that Wrexham's first Premier League campaign was already earning comparisons to Ipswich Town's 1961-62 title win and Brian Clough's Nottingham Forest — while being careful to note that Wrexham, at the time of writing, sat second. That footnote is out of date already. With Arsenal and Manchester City both without midweek fixtures over the Christmas period, three points against West Ham was enough to send Wrexham top of the Premier League table on Christmas Day, in their first season in the competition's history. Meredith, watching from the press box, was careful with his framing even in a moment that invited hyperbole: "Top of the table on the 25th of December means precisely nothing about where this team finishes in May. It means everything about how far this club has already come." A West Ham player, asked in the mixed zone what it was like facing a promoted side sitting top of the league, put it more bluntly: "They don't play like a team that's supposed to be here for the first time. Somebody should tell them that."

Wrexham are back in action for the trip to Ipswich Town on Boxing Day week, with the fixture list showing no sign of slowing down before the New Year. For a club that spent last Christmas fighting for automatic promotion out of the Championship, sitting top of the Premier League twelve months later is the kind of contrast nobody at the Racecourse is taking for granted just yet.

Nico Kopp Man of the Match Again as Wrexham's Kids See Off Wolves to Win a Third Straight Youth Rush Title

A tighter final than the last two, but the same result — and, remarkably, the same name topping the ratings sheet at full-time as it had four hours earlier in the semi.

If Wrexham's academy has made anything look routine this season, it is the closing weeks of the Youth Rush calendar. A 2–0 win over Wolves in the final was a more modest scoreline than the double-digit routs that closed out the previous campaign, but the outcome was never seriously in question. Martin Leon opened the scoring from a tight angle he has been practising in training for weeks, according to academy staff, and Nathaniel Matthews doubled the lead before the hour — his first involvement of a final that, for long stretches, Wolves' back line spent simply trying to survive.

The story of the day, though, belonged between the posts. Nico Kopp, the German goalkeeper who has quietly become the most talked-about name in the academy's development meetings, followed a man-of-the-match display in the afternoon's semi-final with another one in the final — two commanding performances in the space of a single day that left Owen Meredith's notebook short on comparisons. "You don't usually get a goalkeeper man-of-the-match twice in one afternoon," Meredith wrote pitchside. "Kopp made it look almost unremarkable, which is its own kind of remarkable."

Valentino Martini's Hat-Trick From Centre-Back Sends Wrexham's Kids Past Brentford and Into the Final

A centre-back doesn't usually headline a semi-final report. Today, nobody in the press box was arguing with the exception.

Valentino Martini has spent his time in Wrexham's academy being talked about for his reading of the game, not his finishing — a Ball-Playing Defender by trade, more likely to start a move than end one. That reputation took a battering in the best possible way against Brentford, as the Italian teenager scored three times from sat-piece situations and open play alike in a 4–1 semi-final win that sent Wrexham through to a third straight Youth Rush final. Nathan Salaun added the fourth, a tidy finish that will have done his own case for more minutes no harm at all.

Behind it all, Nico Kopp produced the kind of performance that makes academy staff sit up in meetings: a 9.8 rating and the match ball's worth of big saves that kept Brentford's one moment of real quality from becoming two. "Val's not going to become a striker overnight, and that's fine," Jenkins said afterward, smiling. "But that's the calibre of player we're developing across this whole group now — even the ones we build the team around defensively can produce an afternoon like that." Martini himself, still visibly surprised by his own hat-trick, put it more simply: "I don't really know what happened. The ball kept finding me and I kept putting it in."

Jules Collin's Brace Opens a New Youth Rush Campaign With a 3–1 Win Over Crystal Palace

A tighter opener than the last one, with Mika Horn called into real action for the first time all campaign — and answering the call.

Nobody in the camp seemed especially worried about the closer scoreline — not with a semi-final and, potentially, a third straight final still to come before the day was out.

Three Days After Reijnders Broke Their Hearts, Wrexham's Kids Go to the Etihad and Beat Manchester City's Best

Saturday's ninetieth-minute sucker-punch was still fresh when the teamsheets went up for a Carabao Cup quarter-final nobody outside the building expected Wrexham to take seriously. City took it seriously. Wrexham took the result.

Three days is not very long to get over a goal like Tijjani Reijnders'. Owen Meredith was still writing about the Racecourse's stunned final whistle from Saturday's 1–2 when the club bus pulled out for Manchester again, this time for a Carabao Cup quarter-final that plenty of neutrals had already filed under "dead rubber" — the kind of midweek fixture a newly promoted side is expected to use for squad rotation and not much else. Manchester City, notably, did not treat it that way. Pep Guardiola named a team with real senior quality in it, the sort of selection that says a club still wants the trophy rather than one going through the motions. Keeyvon Jenkins, for his part, sent out a Wrexham XI built almost entirely around academy graduates and squad players who rarely feature in the Premier League picture. On paper, four days after a heartbreak against City's actual first team, this looked like the kind of week that could break a promoted club's spirit rather than restore it.

It did the opposite. Juan Cruz Vargas, who has made a habit this season of finding the net from the bench, broke the deadlock in the 40th minute with a finish that owed as much to composure as anything else — the same calm City's more expensive attackers had lacked three days earlier at the other end of the M56. Chido Obi, the teenager who promised in August that Wrexham "wasn't scared of the prestige of this league," made good on the words from the penalty spot in the 66th, sending the away end at the Etihad into a noise usually reserved for occasions considerably bigger than a midweek cup tie. Two goals, a clean sheet, and a Wrexham XI that had barely featured together before kickoff walked off having beaten a City side with real first-team players in it — on the same ground, in the same week, as the club that had just denied them at the death.

Whatever they're building at that club, it's not just the first team." Sauer's Stoppage-Time Penalty Seals It as Wrexham Survive a Scare at Brentford to Go Second

A week after the City heartbreak, Wrexham needed a nervy, error-strewn ninety to keep pace at the top — and got the job done in exactly the manner Jenkins didn't want them to.

If last Saturday's near-miss against Manchester City was Wrexham at their most composed, this was the opposite fixture in almost every respect. Damián Bobadilla put the visitors ahead in the 26th minute with the kind of driven finish from the edge of the box that has become his trademark this season, and for the best part of an hour Wrexham looked in control of a game that, on paper, they were expected to be in control of.
