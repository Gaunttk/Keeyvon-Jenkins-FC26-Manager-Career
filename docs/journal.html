The Carabao Awakening: Wrexham Stroll Into Round Two

Three days after a 4-1 statement at Southampton, Jenkins's side travelled to Preston and barely broke a sweat. The cup is no longer an afterthought at Wrexham.

Before we get to the result — and the result was good — it's worth stepping back and asking a question Wrexham supporters haven't had reason to ask in a long time: what even is this competition to them?

The Carabao Cup has changed its name eleven times since its 1960 founding. The final is still at Wembley. The big clubs still rotate. But for a side like Wrexham — one year into the Championship, still learning what this division costs — it is something else entirely. It is a Tuesday night on the road with a full squad, against opposition that doesn't have your scouting report yet. It is, in other words, a gift.

Keeyvon Jenkins used it like one.

His side travelled to Deepdale three days after dismantling Southampton and played with a composure that will have surprised the home faithful. Not reckless pressing, not desperate defending — structure. Wrexham looked like a team that had been coached. Nineteen minutes in, D. Keillor-Dunn — fresh off his Man of the Match showing on the opening day — converted to make it 1–0. Sixteen minutes later, J. Windass doubled the lead before half-time, and the second half became a rotation exercise. Jenkins rang the changes — Longman on for Broadhead, Rathbone on for Windass, Dobson on for Sheaf — and Preston couldn't find a way back in.

The number that caught my eye wasn't on the scoresheet. It was the expected goals figure: 3.6 for Wrexham against 0.6 for Preston. That is not a flattering margin. That is dominance wearing a modest scoreline. Wrexham created chances they didn't take — the sort of wastefulness that won't be punished in a cup tie but will be against Championship sides with something to prove.

"The Carabao Cup is where you learn who actually wants to play for you."

— Owen Meredith, on the fringe players' auditions at Deepdale

Ismaël Kaboré was the most interesting story of the night. The Burkina Faso international — listed as a right back, deployed as a right back, but clearly itching to get forward — picked up an assist and a 7.4 rating. Buried inside the stat sheet: 16 interceptions from the right back position, anchoring Wrexham's defensive shape while the attack did its work. Jenkins's system rewards the right back's engine. Kaboré might be the engine.

Wrexham have now won six consecutive fixtures across pre-season and competitive play. Round Two awaits.

August 12, 2026 · Preston

First Blood

The cup competition nobody respects. We treated it like it mattered. That's the point.

Three days after Southampton, we went to Deepdale and did it again. Different competition, same result: composure, control, and a clean sheet. I circled August 12 in my notebook before kickoff — first Carabao Cup tie in charge. These are the moments that add up.

Dane got us going at nineteen minutes and I felt the bench exhale. Josh doubled it before half and we went in 2–0 up away from home in a proper cup tie. I rotated in the second half — Longman, Rathbone, Dobson all got minutes — and none of them let us down. That depth is real. Last season it wasn't. That's the difference.

Ismaël was the real story, though. Sixteen interceptions from right back. The man reads the game before it happens — he was always one step ahead of Preston's movement, cutting off angles before they became options. And then he's got the assist on top of that. I've been watching him carefully since pre-season and tonight confirmed what I suspected: if he can maintain that reading at Championship pace, we have something genuinely special in that position.

"Win the cup game. Then win the next one. The season isn't a story yet — it's just a Tuesday night in Preston."

The xG split tells the truth: 3.6 to 0.6. We left goals out there that we shouldn't have, and I've already flagged that with the forwards in the debrief. But the structure held. The press worked. We didn't concede a single shot on target. Arthur barely had a save to make.

Six wins from six across pre-season and competition. That number will stop mattering the moment we drop one. I know that. But tonight, in the dressing room at Deepdale, there was a quiet confidence I haven't felt yet from this group. Not arrogance. Belief. That's what you're building toward.

Round Two. Then Matchday 2. Then we keep going.

— KJ

Wrexham Announce Themselves With a 4-1 Statement on the South Coast

Keillor-Dunn and Moore both score twice, Broadhead pulls every string — but the same defensive lapses that haunted last season showed up again, even in victory.

Nobody in the away end at St Mary's expected this. Not 4-1. Not against a Southampton side many had pencilled in as automatic promotion contenders. But here was Wrexham, twenty minutes from kickoff into a new Championship campaign, playing like a club with something to prove.

Danny Keillor-Dunn was the headline. Two goals, an assist, Man of the Match in a performance that suggested last season's frustrations behind him. Kasey Moore matched him goal for goal. And in behind both of them, Noah Broadhead was the architect — three assists, pulling Southampton's back line apart with a vision that the highlight reel will not do justice to.

Matchday 1 — Southampton (A)
Final Score Southampton 1–4 Wrexham
Scorers Keillor-Dunn (2), Moore (2)
Man of the Match D. Keillor-Dunn
Assists N. Broadhead (3)

It wasn't flawless. Jenkins's press forced turnover after turnover in the final third, but there were stretches — particularly either side of the hour mark — where Southampton walked through a back line that looked unsure of its shape. A 4-1 scoreline flatters the gaps that were there to be exploited. Wrexham made far more of their chances than their opponents did, and on another day this is a different kind of result.

Off the pitch, the business has already started. Ryan Barnett left for Watford in a £1.3m deal — a player who'd given the club steady service on the right flank, but one Jenkins clearly felt he could do without given the depth he's building behind him. Danny Ward and Dan Scarr have both gone up for sale, with Aaron James shipped out on a loan listing. The exits suggest a manager trimming fat before he adds muscle.

"You don't win 4-1 away at a promotion favourite by accident. But you also don't concede the way Wrexham did in the second half by accident either. Jenkins has the attack. The defence is still his unfinished business."

— Red Dragon Dispatch analysis

Next up: Preston North End at home in the Carabao Cup. A trophy that means little to the bigger clubs in this competition might mean something different to a Wrexham side still building belief in itself.

Entry 005
Aug 9, 2026 · Wrexham

4-1 feels great. It also shouldn't have been that close.

A dominant scoreline, a few defensive ghosts, and a roster that finally feels like it's becoming mine.

Four-one away at Southampton. I'll take that result every single week for the rest of my career and not apologize for it. Danny was everywhere — two goals, MOTM, the kind of performance that tells me last season's struggles are behind him. And Broadhead — three assists. Three. The kid sees passes nobody else on that pitch saw.

But I watched the tape back twice already and I keep seeing the same thing: we gave Southampton chances we had no business giving them. A few lapses, a few missed easy chances of our own that could've made this an even bigger statement. We out-played them for long stretches and still let them hang around longer than they deserved to. That's not a one-off. That's a pattern from last year creeping back in on day one.

I'll enjoy this win tonight. Tomorrow I'm back on the defensive shape, because the next team we get sloppy against won't be as generous as we were lucky.

The business side was harder than the match, honestly. Letting Barnett go to Watford for £1.3m — tough call. He never let me down when I called his number, and saying goodbye never gets easier, no matter how many times I tell myself it's the right move for where this squad needs to go. Ward and Scarr are both up for sale now too. Hard conversations, all of them. Some of those wages just don't make sense for what we're building toward.

The Academy gave me some reasons to smile, at least. Four new signings — a couple of Italians, an Argentine, an Englishman barely out of short trousers. None of them are ready. None of them need to be yet. That's the whole point of building a pipeline instead of praying for transfer windows to save you.

Preston in the cup next. Different test, different rhythm. But for tonight — 4-1 on the south coast. I'll sleep alright.

— KJ

Thirty-Six Names. Eight Ghosts. One American Dream.

Jenkins has catalogued his squad, formalised the academy, and sent eight loan players back to their owners. What he's left with tells the story of a club still becoming itself.

There's a moment at every pre-season where a manager stops looking at the names he has and starts thinking about the ones he doesn't. Keeyvon Jenkins hit that moment somewhere around the third week of July, surrounded by fitness data and a spreadsheet that told him the truth he already suspected.

Eight players came off the board. Stephen Revan, Elliott Ashfield, Ethan Cannon, Jack Mullin, Jordan Hardie, Tengis Faal, Tom O'Connor — all back to parent clubs. So, quietly, was Coady. Not all of them were squad players. Some of them were the squad. Jenkins knew this when he arrived. He inherited a survival team, not a Championship team. The loans papered over the gaps. Now the gaps are exposed.

What's left is a squad with a heartbeat — Ben Sheaf at 73 overall runs everything through the middle, and on the right flank, Ismaël Kaboré on loan from Manchester City is the kind of player who makes opposition wingers nervous. At the back, there are pieces. Whether there's a unit is a different question.

The academy is real now. Six players, formalised, with development plans active. The headline is a sixteen-year-old goalkeeper named M. Webber — 58 now, with a ceiling projection that touches 94. Clubs with Premier League ambitions don't casually generate potential like that. Jenkins will want to keep him close. The wolves always circle.

"The board want promotion. First. Automatic. Jenkins smiled when they told him, the way you smile when someone says something you can't argue with but aren't quite sure you believe."

— Owen Meredith, Red Dragon Dispatch

The Championship doesn't care about project timelines. It doesn't care that Jenkins inherited a squad built for survival. It will hand out 46 fixtures and wait to see who breaks first. Right now, Wrexham are 36 names, a handful of bright spots, and a manager who presses high and sleeps fine. The season starts soon. We'll learn what that's worth.

Late July 2026 · Wrexham

I know this squad now. That's not the same as trusting it.

Pre-season is done. The picture is clear. I just don't love all of it.

Thirty-six names. I know them now — their ceiling, their floor, their contract length, which ones are fading and which ones have something left to prove. That's what pre-season is for. Not the friendlies you never actually play. The audit.

Eight players left when their loans expired. I knew it was coming. Doesn't make the depth chart any easier to read. We're thin in places I can't hide. Wide areas. A third centre-back if one of the starters goes down. Depth at nine. The kind of problems you solve in windows, not on training pitches.

Sheaf is the one I'm building everything around. Seventy-three overall, and the number doesn't tell you the whole story — it doesn't tell you that he reads the game a beat before everyone else in this squad, that he's the one who'll keep us organized when the press breaks down and the other team runs at us. He's the heartbeat. I told him that. He shrugged. Good.

Kaboré is on loan from City. Right back, 72. There's something special there — his recovery pace, the way he attacks the overlap without losing his defensive shape. If he has a strong year, Man City pull him back. That's the risk I accepted when I took the loan. It's still the right call.

Webber. Sixteen years old. GK ceiling projection: 94. I wrote that down. I'm going to tape it somewhere I can see it on a bad day.

The academy paperwork is done. Six players. Plans in place. The two GKs — Webber and Gassner — are polar opposites in how they approach the game, and that's good. Healthy competition starts early. Lord at left back has a week until his next rating tick. Hak on the right wing is fifteen and already showing instincts that take most wingers five extra years to develop. Dijkgraaf is the steady one — doesn't flash, just works.

The board want automatic promotion. I wrote that down too, right next to Webber's ceiling. A reminder that this club — these owners — are not interested in the journey. They want the destination. I respect that, actually. I want the same thing. I just know how much can go wrong between now and May.

The first match comes soon. I'm ready. I think we're ready. We'll find out.

— KJ

Moore Lights Up Madrid. But It's Lord Who's Turning Heads Back Home.

Two competitions, four wins, no defeats — Jenkins' preseason is going perfectly. Which is exactly when you should start worrying.

The scoreboard in Madrid told one story. Wrexham AFC 5, AD Ceuta FC 0. The kind of result that makes highlights packages and gets quoted in board meetings. But the more interesting number — the one that kept getting mentioned in the press area afterwards — was three.

Three assists. One goal. Keeyvon Moore, the club's wide forward, was everywhere against Ceuta on July 22nd, operating in the pockets between lines in a way that made the Spanish side look like they'd never prepared for anything. Keillor-Dunn converted twice from his service. Cadamarteri and O'Brien got on the scoresheet. Moore took the Man of the Match award and looked faintly bored by the whole thing.

Four days earlier, Wrexham had dismissed Bradford City 4-0 in the European International Cup — Broadhead with two goals, Moore again involved, the system clicking with a fluency you rarely see before a ball has been kicked in anger. Jenkins watched both performances with the arms-folded calm of a man who is trying very hard not to look pleased with himself.

EIC vs AD Ceuta FC W 5–0 · Keillor-Dunn (2), Moore, Cadamarteri, O'Brien

The youth picture is more complicated and, frankly, more compelling. Wrexham's Academy side has ground their way to the Youth Tournament final through sheer stubbornness — a composed 2-1 win over Hull City in the quarter-final, then a nervy, chaotic 3-2 in extra time against Millwall in the semi. Lord has scored four goals across both matches, emerging as the tournament's standout forward with a tenacity that the first team's coaching staff will not have missed. Goalkeeper Gassner was named Man of the Match against Hull — which, for a youth tournament in July, is the kind of detail that gets filed away and remembered in three years.

"Pre-season tells you what you want to see. It almost never tells you what you need to know."

— Owen Meredith, Red Dragon Dispatch

The Huesca fixture looms. SD Huesca arrive in the EIC Group Stage also unbeaten, also with something to prove. For the first time this preseason, Wrexham will face a side whose training ground conversations sound a lot like theirs. Jenkins will know this. His teams are built to dominate — the real question, always, is how they respond when someone refuses to be dominated.

Ask that question in August. For now, the scorelines are very pretty indeed.

Nine goals in two games. And I'm still thinking about a kid named Lord.

The first team is clicking. The academy is grinding. Both are exactly what I asked for.

5-0 in Madrid. I keep saying it to myself, not to celebrate — to check whether it feels real yet. It does not. Ceuta were poor, and we were clinical, but there were passages in that second half where the system ran itself. Players finding the right pockets, the press triggers happening without anyone calling for them, Moore just... drifting into space and making things happen like it cost him nothing. That's what this style looks like when it works. That's the vision.

Moore is the story of this preseason for the first team. Goal, three assists, Man of the Match, looked like he was playing at half-pace. I need to watch how he handles an opponent who doesn't gift him that space — because Huesca won't. But right now? He's exactly the player I thought we had when I watched the film in June.

Broadhead was excellent against Bradford. Two goals, movement intelligent, held the line well for the press. Keillor-Dunn getting on the scoresheet twice in Madrid tells me his confidence is building. Cadamarteri and O'Brien doing their jobs. This front line can hurt people in the Championship. I believe that.

But the truth is I spent most of tonight thinking about Lord.

Four goals in two youth matches, and the one that ended the Millwall game in extra time — I've watched the clip twice. The kid didn't celebrate. Just turned and jogged back. That's either composure or something better than composure. I've put a note in with the development staff. I want to see him in a first-team training environment before the season starts.

Gassner in goal for the youth team against Hull — Man of the Match. Two things can be true: the scoreline was narrow, and the keeper was the reason. I want to know more about him.

Huesca next. Both undefeated, both playing well. This is the first test that will actually tell me something. I'm ready.

Jenkins Builds His Backroom. But the Numbers Don't Lie.

Four coaches hired, a High Pressing identity locked in — but gaping holes in the staff quality tell the real story of where Wrexham are starting from.

Keeyvon Jenkins has never been a man who takes long to make decisions. Four coaches. Four days. Backroom assembled.

The appointments tell you everything about what Jenkins believes in. Carter Fowler comes in as Head Attack Coach — four stars in his specialism, mobile, progressive, the kind of coach who thrives in a press-heavy system. Riley Gardner takes the midfield brief and is quietly the most complete member of the new staff — four stars across attack and midfield, four in defence too, earning the highest wage on the coaching sheet at $8,962 a week. Jenkins clearly identified him as the linchpin.

Morgan Ford takes the defensive brief at $2,926 a week — the cheapest hire and the most concerning. Defence is where Wrexham's ideal target sits at a staggering 19 stars, and Ford's four-star rating barely dents that gap. For a club that conceded their way to 24th place, this is a problem Jenkins will need to address the moment budget allows.

Lee Gale rounds out the staff in goal, four stars in his specialism, reliable if unspectacular.

"The tactical vision is High Pressing. That's not a surprise — it's who Jenkins is. The question is whether this squad has the legs to run his system for 46 Championship matches."

— Red Dragon Dispatch analysis

What the coaching screen doesn't show is personality, belief, or the ability to drag a bottom-of-the-table squad through a Wednesday night in February. That part is on Jenkins himself. The backroom is built. Now comes the harder work.

Early July 2026 · Wrexham

I hired four coaches. I needed forty.

The staff is in. The gaps are embarrassing. We press anyway.

Gardner was the first call I made. Didn't even look at the budget. When you find someone who can coach attack, midfield and defend at four stars each, you pay the $8,962 and you thank God he picked up the phone. Riley's the glue. Every good coaching staff has one — the person who holds the whole thing together when the system breaks down at 2-0 down in the 70th minute. That's him.

Fowler's exactly what I wanted up front. Aggressive, forward-thinking, understands pressing triggers. He and Gardner are going to be a good pairing on the training pitch.

Ford. I like Morgan. Good man, four stars in defence, works hard. But I'm staring at a target of 19 defensive coaching stars and we're sitting at four. That number keeps me up at night more than the league table does. We gave up goals last season like they were going out of fashion and I've got a defensive coaching setup that's closer to a League Two outfit than a Championship promotion contender.

Marcus called tonight. Asked how the team was. I told him we were 24th. He said "so you've got nowhere to go but up, Dad." Kid's twelve going on forty.

High Pressing is the vision. It's who I am, it's what I know, and honestly — it's the only way a squad with this level of individual quality gap can compete in this division. You can't out-talent Championship clubs on a Wrexham budget. But you can out-work them. You can press the life out of them in the first twenty minutes and make them hate playing against you.

That's the plan. It's a thin plan. But it's ours.

Jenkins Arrives. The Clock Is Already Ticking.

The American takes the Wrexham job with the club rooted to the bottom of the Championship and a board demanding the impossible.

There was something almost cinematic about it — which, given who owns this football club, felt entirely appropriate.

Keeyvon Jenkins walked into his Wrexham AFC unveiling on June 30th in a grey V-neck and red tie, looking less like a man inheriting a crisis and more like someone who'd been planning this moment for years. The former USMNT midfielder, 38 years old and carrying the kind of quiet confidence that only comes from having played at the highest level, sat behind the microphones at STōK Cae Ras and said all the right things.

The official club account posted the press conference clip within the hour. 1.98K likes before sundown.

Situation at Appointment
League Position 24th — Bottom
Job Security Safe
Board Mandate Automatic Promotion
FA Cup Objective Round of 16 — In Progress

What the post didn't mention — what nobody wanted to say out loud on day one — is that Wrexham are 24th in the EFL Championship. Bottom. The Hollywood dream that began in the National League, that carried this club through three consecutive promotions and a documentary series watched by millions, has run headfirst into the reality of English football's second tier.

Reynolds and McElhenney have made bold calls before. Appointing an American manager with no top-flight European head coaching experience is either the boldest yet — or the most desperate.

"I fell in love with Wales during my Swansea years. When Ryan and Rob called, I didn't think twice. This club has unfinished business."

— Keeyvon Jenkins, unveiling press conference

The board's mandate is unambiguous: automatic promotion. Not a playoff push. Not consolidation. Up. This season. Jenkins inherits a dressing room that needs belief more than tactics right now. Whether "The Hawk" — as American soccer fans have called him since his Fulham days — can deliver that in time remains the only question that matters in North Wales this summer.

The fans in the replies were cautiously optimistic. One comment stood out among the emojis: "What's the latest on that story?"

Good question, Jerry. Good question.

They welcomed me with emojis.

First day. Last place. One shot.

They welcomed me with a social post and a couple of emojis. 1.98K likes. Not bad for a kid from Olathe.

I sat in that press room for twenty minutes before they let me in. Red and white everywhere. The EFL Championship badge on the backdrop — still surreal to see it. Last time I was in Wales it was the Premier League. Different world.

The board made the expectations clear before I'd even unpacked. Automatic promotion. Not playoffs. Automatic. And we're currently sitting 24th in the Championship. Dead last. Reynolds didn't say it directly but the message was obvious — this isn't a rebuild timeline, it's a rescue mission.

Danielle texted me during the presser. "How's Wales?" I typed back "Diolch" just to make her laugh.

The FA Cup objective is already in progress, which at least gives me something to feel good about. But I'll be honest — I barely slept last night. I pulled up the squad on my laptop around 2am just staring at it. There's something here. I can feel it. But 24th is 24th.

Gipper. That's what they're calling me apparently. The appellative EA gave me in the game. I've been called worse.

First training session tomorrow. These players don't know me yet. By the end of the week, they will.
