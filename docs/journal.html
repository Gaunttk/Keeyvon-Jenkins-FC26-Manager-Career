Kudus Silences the Racecourse Twice as Wrexham Suffer Their First Premier League Defeat

Four matchdays into the top flight, the unbeaten run and the perfect home record both ended on the same Friday night — and one of them had never happened to Keeyvon Jenkins in the league before, home or away.

For three matchdays, the Racecourse Ground had been the one constant in a Premier League campaign that everyone connected to Wrexham kept describing, carefully, as "too good to be true." Then Mohammed Kudus arrived and made the case that it was. His first goal, in the 42nd minute, was a finish so unhurried it looked rehearsed — cutting inside Jorthy Mokio, who had otherwise been the best player on the pitch in a rare bright spot for a home side that spent long stretches of the second half chasing shadows, and curling a shot past Arthur Okonkwo that the goalkeeper barely moved for. The second, in the 63rd, was more of the same: patient, inevitable, delivered by a player who looked, for ninety minutes, like he was competing in a different league to everyone else wearing red.

Jenkins didn't reach for excuses afterward, which by now is close to a signature of his post-match routine whether the result is good or bad. "We got beaten by a better team tonight, and a player who's better than anyone we had on the pitch," he said, flatly, in a press room that had grown used to him leaving with a smile. "That's allowed to happen in this league. What isn't allowed is us forgetting how to play the way we've been playing because of one bad night." Andrés Gómez, who had a quieter afternoon than his 6.7 rating even suggests, put it more bluntly in the mixed zone: "He scored twice and we still had chances. We just didn't take them. That's on us, not just him."

The historical footnote will matter more to the people who keep the club's ledgers than it did to anyone in the away end celebrating Tottenham's third-choice striker option finding this kind of form, but it's real: this is the first home defeat of Jenkins's Wrexham tenure in any league competition — the only previous loss at the Racecourse under him came in last August's Carabao Cup exit to Blackburn Rovers, a cup tie the club treated as a squad-rotation exercise at the time. There is no such excuse available tonight. Wrexham fielded close to a full-strength side, and a full-strength side lost.

Owen Meredith's column has spent four matchdays wondering aloud whether a squad built this quickly, around this many teenagers, could survive contact with the league's genuine quality without a stumble. Tonight supplied the answer, and it wasn't a flattering one, though it also wasn't the humiliation some of the more excitable corners of the visiting support seemed to be hoping for pre-match. Wrexham had spells of real football in this — Rio Ngumoha in particular kept testing a Tottenham left side that never fully solved him — but spells of real football against a front-runner for the league's best individual talent this month were never going to be enough on their own. The Racecourse crowd, to its credit, stayed until the final whistle and gave the players an ovation that had more resignation than anger in it. Whether that patience survives a second bad night in a row, with Chelsea waiting a week from Saturday, is the more interesting question than tonight's scoreline on its own.

The First One That Hurts

Four matchdays of a perfect home record, and then a Friday night that reminded me what this league is actually for.

I've lost plenty of games in my life, as a player and as a manager, and I've told plenty of dressing rooms that a loss doesn't undo the work that came before it. Tonight is the first time I've had to say it and actually feel the sentence testing itself as it left my mouth. Wolves, the draw at Sheffield United, Bournemouth — four weeks of building something that felt, for the first time in this job, like it might hold up against anything the Premier League could throw at it. Then Kudus happened twice in one night and reminded all of us, myself included, exactly what "anything" can look like at this level.

What actually bothers me isn't the scoreline. It's that this is the first time I've lost a league match at home in this entire job — Championship included, and that's counting fifty-some matches at the Racecourse before tonight. The only other loss we've taken in this building was a cup tie I half-rotated the side for. This one, we didn't rotate for anything. We played our best available team and a better player beat us. I told the group after the whistle that the standard doesn't move because of one result, and I believe that, but I also know that the way we respond to the next one says more about what we've actually built here than four wins in a row ever could.

Four weeks ago I wrote in here that I was terrified before the Wolves match and we won anyway. Tonight I wasn't terrified. I was confident, and we lost. I'm not sure yet which version of me I trust more.

— KJ

The Armband Changes Hands as Wrexham Close Out a Window That Sent Its Kids Out to Learn

Deadline day brought a new goalkeeper, a captain nobody voted for but everybody expected, and two teenagers packed off to Wales's near neighbours to find out what they're actually made of.

There was no press conference for it, no ceremonial handover in front of the cameras, but by the time Wrexham's transfer business closed on deadline day the captaincy had quietly moved from Dominic Hyam's armband to Callum Doyle's, and nobody inside the Racecourse seemed to think it needed announcing. Hyam, the Scotland international who'd worn the armband since two months into his Wrexham career, completed a £2.6m move to Rangers earlier in the day — a sale that closed a chapter on the club's promotion-era spine as cleanly as any transfer this window. Doyle, 22, already the better player by most statistical measures this season, inherits a dressing room that has been trending toward him for months anyway.

"Dom was here when this club still had one foot in the Championship in its head, even after we went up," Jenkins said of the departing captain, choosing his words with more care than deadline-day platitudes usually require. "You don't replace what he gave this group. You just find the next person who can carry it, and I think that's obvious to everyone in that dressing room already." Doyle, informed of the decision after training rather than in any grander setting, was characteristically understated about it: "I'll just keep doing what I've been doing. If that's leading, fine. I'm not going to start giving speeches."

The captaincy change came bundled with two more paths out of the building. Carlos Macia, a 17-year-old central midfielder signed from Villarreal for £2.0m, barely had time to unpack before being loaned straight to Swansea City for the season — a 70/30 wage-split arrangement that leaves Wrexham covering the smaller share while a player Jenkins's staff rate as the long-term answer to the club's ongoing shortage of homegrown central midfield options goes and actually plays first-team football somewhere else. Mason Webber, the academy goalkeeper promoted in an emergency back in March, followed him out the door on an even longer arrangement: two years at Turkish side Samsunspor, same wage split, same logic. "There's no version of this where either of them plays regularly for us right now," Jenkins explained. "So they go and play regularly for someone else instead. That's not a demotion. That's the job."

The incoming piece of business that actually strengthens the first-team squad immediately is Bernt Klaverboer, a 20-year-old Dutch goalkeeper signed from Burnley for £6.2m to sit behind Arthur Okonkwo — cover the club needed the moment Okonkwo's tricep injury exposed how thin things were between the posts with Webber still raw and now, as of this week, on another continent's payroll. Klaverboer's kicking numbers alone (84, comfortably the best distribution in the goalkeeping department) had recruitment staff talking about him as more than a backup from the first scouting report, though his shot-stopping is described internally as still catching up to his distribution.

You don't build a club by keeping every good young player close and hoping they develop by osmosis. Sometimes the best thing you can do for a seventeen-year-old is put him somewhere he has to sink or swim.

— Keeyvon Jenkins, on the Macia and Webber loans

Taken as a whole, it's a deadline day that tells a fairly coherent story about where this club thinks it is: financially secure enough to spend £6.2m on a squad goalkeeper without blinking, patient enough to loan out a £2.0m signing the same afternoon it signed him, and stable enough that a captaincy change everyone saw coming barely registered as news. Whether that stability survives its first real Premier League test — Tottenham arrive inside the fortnight — is a different question. But a club that can lose its captain, ship out two prospects, and add a goalkeeper for £6.2m in a single day without so much as a ripple through the dressing room is not, whatever the American-owners jokes still doing the rounds online might suggest, a club in the middle of an identity crisis. It looks, increasingly, like one that knows exactly what it's building.

Fruk's Brace and a Cacace Masterclass Keep Wrexham Unbeaten Against Bournemouth

Three Premier League games, seven points, and a full-back still finding his feet in a new position who just delivered the best ninety minutes anyone in this building has seen from him.

There was nothing scrappy about this one. Toni Fruk scored twice — the first a well-worked finish just after the hour, the second a penalty with seven minutes left that took any remaining doubt out of the afternoon — and Wrexham controlled a 2–0 win over Bournemouth from a position of comfort that has been rare for a newly promoted side three matchdays into a Premier League campaign. But the story of the day, by general agreement inside the Racecourse press box, wasn't the scoreline. It was Liberato Cacace, still not three weeks into a right-back conversion nobody outside this club fully understood at the time, delivering a 7.9-rated Man of the Match performance that made Jenkins's decision to move his record signing across the pitch look less like an experiment and more like a plan that had already worked.

The move itself has been the subject of a small cottage industry of second-guessing since it was made public a fortnight ago, and Cacace didn't pretend he hadn't heard it. "I've played left-back my whole career, for every club, for my country," he said afterward, still in his kit, sweat not yet dried. "You don't just switch that off in three weeks. But the gaffer sat me down and told me why, and I trusted it, and today I think people saw why he trusts it too." It was the kind of quote that could have sounded defensive from a lesser performance. From a man who'd just recorded the best rating of any Wrexham player in three Premier League matches, it read instead like a receipt being collected.

Rio Ngumoha set up Fruk's opener with an assist that drew applause from a home end that has been steadily warming to a teenager who spent his first month in Wales carrying a transfer fee that would have bought this club's entire promotion-winning squad. Bournemouth, for their part, never really threatened a way back into it, and their own manager admitted as much in the away dugout after the whistle, telling Cherries reporters that his side had "arrived thinking about the size of the occasion instead of the size of the pitch." Fruk's penalty in the 83rd minute, won by a driving Ngumoha run that the Bournemouth back line simply couldn't match for pace, was more formality than statement by that point — the game had been decided in the buildup play long before the spot kick was ever needed.

Three games, seven points, and an academy-and-reinvention project that keeps producing performances that look better in hindsight than they did on the team sheet the morning of. It fits a pattern Meredith has tracked in this column before: Jenkins does not appear to make a single positional decision this club considers safe, and so far the club has not paid for a single one of them. Whether that holds up against sterner opposition than an out-of-sorts Bournemouth side is the question for next month — Tottenham and Chelsea both arrive inside the next fortnight, and neither will be as generous with space in behind as Bournemouth were today. For now, Cacace's conversion is no longer a risk anyone in this building has to defend, and the man himself, asked whether he wanted his old shirt number back, laughed and said only: "Ask me again in May."

Veleten Runs the Show and a Loan Returnee Announces Himself as Wrexham Thump Huddersfield 5–0

A second-string XI took Huddersfield apart in the Carabao Cup, and the afternoon's headline wasn't the two Chido Obi goals — it was the striker who'd spent the summer at Cheltenham Town reminding everyone he still exists.

Vladyslav Veleten took the match ball home after a goal and two assists steered a rotated Wrexham side past Huddersfield Town without a flicker of resistance from the visitors, but the name on more lips in the mixed zone afterward was Mo Faal. Signed for a club-record fee before this project even had a name, loaned out to Cheltenham Town for a season while a queue of younger, cheaper strikers formed ahead of him, Faal walked back into a matchday squad and straight into the starting XI, and repaid the recall with a goal and an assist in a man-of-the-match-adjacent shift that at least reopens a conversation most had considered closed.

"I never stopped believing I could do this at this level," Faal said afterward, still catching his breath by the tunnel. "Cheltenham was good for me — I needed games, I needed to feel wanted somewhere. But this is my club. I never wanted to be somewhere else." It's the kind of line every player says on the way back from a loan, whether they mean it or not. What made this one land was the manager backing it up unprompted a few minutes later.

Chido Obi's brace either side of half-time did the bulk of the damage on the scoresheet, and Lewis O'Brien added a fifth from midfield before the hour, but this was, in the end, a rout built on a Cup team finding its rhythm early and never letting Huddersfield settle. Obi, asked about sharing a forward line with a teammate he'd never actually played alongside before kickoff, shrugged off the newness of it: "Mo knows where to be. I don't have to think about it, I just play the pass." Andrés Cuenca and Elijah Dijkstra both made their first appearances in a Wrexham shirt since their summer arrivals, low-key introductions to a match that had long stopped being competitive by the time either was needed — Cuenca in particular drawing a nod of approval from the bench for a block on the one occasion Huddersfield threatened to break forward with numbers.

The visiting manager, for his part, was gracious in defeat in a way that only a side four levels removed from this one in ambition can afford to be. "You could see the gap in quality from the first minute," he told Huddersfield's local press. "Some of what they're doing on the training ground, we're not doing yet. That's not an excuse, it's just where both clubs are." It was a rare moment of a visiting voice validating, rather than disputing, the story Wrexham has been telling about itself all summer.

Jenkins, asked directly whether Faal had done enough to force his way back into Premier League contention, wouldn't commit to anything beyond the obvious: "He's given me a decision to make that I didn't think I'd have to make this month. That's a good problem." For a club that has spent this entire window trying to get younger, it's a strange kind of subplot — the one player forcing his way back into the picture is the one who's been here the longest.

Wrexham's Teenagers Make It Back-to-Back Youth Rush Titles With an 8–0 Demolition of Tottenham

A semi-final squeaked past New Castle in the morning. By the evening final, Wrexham's academy had turned into the most one-sided story in the whole competition.

There is a version of the Youth Academy Rush Tournament where a club like Wrexham, still finding its feet in the Premier League proper, treats the whole thing as an afterthought — reserve fixtures for the kids who aren't quite ready, filed somewhere behind the real business of Saturday afternoons. Whoever is writing that story clearly hasn't been watching Wrexham's academy side, who followed up May's maiden Youth Rush title by winning the whole thing again, and did it in a final so lopsided that by the hour mark the only remaining question was whether Tottenham's teenagers would touch the ball in Wrexham's half at all.

Stéphane Bertrand, fifteen years old and barely nine days removed from arriving out of a French academy scouting trip Wrexham's recruitment staff had been quietly running all summer, scored a hat-trick. Jules Collin, signed from the same pipeline a fortnight earlier, put together two goals and three assists and walked off with Man of the Match honours in a final his club won 8–0. Juan Cruz Vargas, the Argentine winger who scored in last season's final too, got two more. Ben Forster chipped in a goal and two assists of his own. Between them, four academy prospects with a combined age barely into their sixties produced a scoreline that wouldn't look out of place in a video game set to easy mode — except somebody at Tottenham's academy has to explain this one at Monday training.

Whatever the ledger says about Wrexham's transfer strategy this window, the early return on the academy line items is not subtle.

Soria's Early Strike and a New German Goalkeeper's Debut Send Wrexham Past New Castle

A tight, low-key semi-final produced one goal, one clean sheet, and a first standout performance from a seventeen-year-old signed nine days earlier.

Compared to what followed it in the evening, Wrexham's 1–0 win over New Castle was a scrappy, forgettable footnote — the kind of semi-final that exists purely to get a team to the game that actually matters. But it is worth pausing on, if only because the player who kept it that way wasn't one of the names Wrexham fans have spent the summer reading about. Nico Kopp, the German goalkeeper signed out of academy scouting less than two weeks ago, took Man of the Match honours in his first meaningful action in a Wrexham shirt, doing just enough behind a narrow 1–0 lead to keep New Castle out.

The goal itself came from Marco Soria, the Argentine winger whose name has been a fixture on this competition's scoresheet since last season's run to the title. It was enough. Nobody at the club is pretending it was a classic, and nobody needed it to be — not with a final against Tottenham waiting the same day.

Fruk Cancels Out Bramall Lane Roar as Wrexham Hold Sheffield United to a Draw

A point on the road against a team that hasn't finished outside the top six in living memory. Unspectacular, unbeaten, and — on the evidence of the summer's business — exactly the kind of result this new-look Wrexham is built to grind out.

Bramall Lane does not do quiet afternoons, and it didn't do one on Saturday either, but for the second successive Premier League match it was a Wrexham player putting the ball in the net that did most of the talking. Toni Fruk, deployed just off the front two nights after his teammates were still being asked about a summer of ins-and-outs that has left half the dressing room barely on nodding terms with itself, found the 67th minute and an equaliser that turned a losing afternoon into a share of the points. It wasn't pretty. It wasn't supposed to be.

New arrivals were everywhere in the visitors' XI, which given the week Wrexham has just had was unavoidable. Damián Bobadilla and Jorthy Mokio shielded a back four playing its second game with Liberato Cacace in an unfamiliar shirt number — more on that below — and both did enough to keep the concession count to one. Max Cleworth, rarely mentioned in these pages, was the pick of the afternoon at the back with a 7.1 rating that quietly did more to earn the point than the headline equaliser. At the other end, Toni Fruk's goal was his fourth of the season in all competitions, and the rating collection across the pitch — mostly clustered in the high sixes — read like a squad still working out exactly who it is with three new signings and two departures inside a single week.

The Ledger and the Long Game: Inside Wrexham's Youth-First Summer

Every deal this window has pointed the same direction. Follow the paper trail and a philosophy starts to look less like an accident and more like a plan.

There is a filing cabinet somewhere in the Racecourse's administrative wing — Owen Meredith has never seen it, but he imagines it, because someone in this building has clearly been keeping one — where every transfer this summer gets logged against a single question: does this make the squad younger, or does it not. Read the ledger cold, without context, and the pattern all but writes the headline for you. Paul Mullin, 31, the man whose goals dragged this club up two divisions and into the room where these decisions now get made, sold to Çaykur Rizespor for a fee that wouldn't buy a Championship squad player's boots. George Thomason, also 31 in football years if not in his passport, moved on to Club Brugge. In their place: a twenty-year-old winger from Athletic Bilbao's academy pipeline, three teenagers from scouting trips through France and Germany that most supporters didn't know were happening until the names showed up on the club website, and a full-back who, at 25, has just been asked to relearn his job on the other side of the pitch because the manager decided that's where the squad needed him more.

Start with the boring one, because it tells you the most. Liberato Cacace has played left-back his entire senior career — for Wellington Phoenix, for Sint-Truiden, for Empoli, for New Zealand, and for Wrexham since the club's record-fee arrival gave this ledger its first real signature purchase. On the 15th of August, with no injury forcing the issue and no crisis at right-back to paper over, Jenkins moved him there anyway. It is the kind of call clubs usually only make when they're desperate. Wrexham made it when they weren't, which is either supreme confidence in a coaching staff's ability to reshape a full international mid-career, or it is exactly what it looks like from the outside: a manager treating his best defender like clay rather than a finished product, because clay is what a squad built to keep improving needs its best players to be.

Then there's the money, which in the end tells the same story from a different angle. Nobody at this newspaper has ever accused Keeyvon Jenkins of sentimentality in the transfer market, but even by his standards this window has been ruthless in its efficiency. Leo Sauer, twenty years old and already carrying five appearances' worth of Premier League pace figures that make Wrexham's recruitment staff look clairvoyant, arrived from Athletic Bilbao for a fee that several continental outlets this week described, not unkindly, as "curiously low" for a player of his ceiling. Mullin's exit fee, by contrast, would raise eyebrows for being generous if it weren't clear the club simply wanted the wage bill gone more than it wanted the last possible pound. Add the academy signings — Stéphane Bertrand and Jules Collin arriving out of France, Nico Kopp out of Germany, none of them older than seventeen, all of them acquired for academy-tier fees that will look like nothing at all if even one of the three develops the way their potential ratings suggest they might — and the pattern isn't subtle anymore. This is a club buying low on ceiling and selling high on relief, and doing it on repeat.

None of this happens in isolation from what's been said in this column before. Wrexham's academy graduates breaking through against marquee money spent elsewhere has been a running thread here since before the club even reached the Premier League, and this window is the loudest single statement yet of which side of that argument Jenkins has picked. It is worth saying plainly what several football writers across three countries have now said in print this week, more or less independently of each other: whatever else you make of the American in the technical area, he has become one of the shrewder operators in this division's transfer market. Under market on the buys, at or above it on the sells, and a squad getting visibly younger while somehow also getting better. Whether that philosophy survives its first real relegation scare, or its first star academy graduate turning down a new contract to force a move himself, is a question for another column. For now, the ledger balances, and it balances in Wrexham's favour.

We're not trying to win this season by buying the finished article. We're trying to build a squad that's better in three years than it is today, and every deal we do gets measured against that, not against what looks good in August.

— Keeyvon Jenkins, on this summer's transfer strategy

Fruk and Ngumoha Sink Wolves as Wrexham Win Their First Premier League Match

A hundred and sixty-two years after the club was founded, Wrexham beat Wolves 2–0 at the Racecourse. The scoreline was the easy part. Believing it happened at all is the harder one.

Fruk works the ball forward in the buildup that led to Wrexham's opener.

There is a version of this story that writes itself in cliché — the non-league club, the Hollywood money, the fairy tale complete. Owen Meredith has written that version before and he's tired of it. So here is the other version: Wrexham beat Wolves 2–0 on Saturday, in the Premier League, because Toni Fruk found half a yard on the edge of the box in the 13th minute and buried it, and because Rio Ngumoha — the £22.5m teenager who spent the whole of preseason being asked to justify the fee — ran onto a ball in the 49th minute and finished like he'd been doing it at this level for years. Man of the Match, first appearance of consequence. Three points on the board before September.

Ayden Heaven, another product of a Manchester academy conveyor belt Wrexham has been quietly raiding all summer, started at centre-back and didn't put a foot wrong — a 7.2 rating on his full Premier League debut that nobody outside the building will remember by Christmas, but that the building itself will. Elsewhere, the story belonged to a teenager who hasn't kicked a competitive ball yet: Chido Obi, signed from Manchester United's academy and still working his way up the striker pecking order, used his pre-match media duties to announce, more or less unprompted, that Wrexham "is here, and we are here to stay, and we are here to win," and that the club was not scared of this league's prestige. Jenkins, asked to respond, didn't hedge. He backed it, word for word.

It is easy, and a little cheap, to mock an eighteen-year-old for saying the quiet part out loud before he's started a single top-flight game. It is harder to argue with him after the final whistle. An American coaching a Welsh club that spent eleven years in non-league football just won its opening Premier League fixture, and the loudest voice in the building afterward belonged to a teenager who wasn't scared to say so beforehand. Whether that's confidence or a hostage to fortune is a question for the next twenty-four matchdays. For one Saturday in August, it was just true.

The Top Flight, For Real This Time

Five weeks, five departures, six new faces, and one final whistle nobody in this building will forget.

Last time I wrote one of these, we were coming off a youth tournament win and I still hadn't fully processed that we were about to play in the Premier League. That's changed. In the five weeks since, we let Revan, Coady, Hardie, Lee, and young Ashfield move on, and brought in Belghazi, Barbieri, Ngumoha, Heaven, Dijkstra, and this week Andrés Cuenca out of Barcelona's academy — a fee that would have bought half our old League Two squad for a nineteen-year-old centre-back who hasn't kicked a ball for us yet. That's the scale of things now. Three preseason friendlies, three wins, not a single goal conceded in more than one match. And then Saturday.

I've managed in front of bigger stadiums than the Racecourse. I coached in a World Cup as a player. None of it felt like the fifteen minutes before kickoff against Wolves. Fruk settled everybody down with that finish in the thirteenth minute, and Rio — who's spent two months carrying a transfer fee around like a backpack full of bricks — scored the goal that told me he's going to be alright. Heaven at the back looked like he'd played fifty of these. And Chido, God love him, stood in front of the cameras before a ball was kicked and told the world we weren't scared. I backed him in that press conference without blinking, and I meant every word, but I'll admit here what I wouldn't say to a reporter: I was terrified. Wrexham's never played a Premier League match before today. Now we have, and we won it.

I've been scared before games as a player. I don't remember ever being scared before one as a manager. Today I was both, and we won anyway.

— KJ

Unbeaten Summer Wrapped Up as Amrizi Steals the Show Against Levante

Three friendlies, three wins, and it was the free-agent forward everybody let go, not the £22.5m kid, who closed out preseason with the best number on the board.

Wrexham finished their European International Cup preseason campaign unbeaten, beating Levante 2–0 at a neutral venue to close out a summer that saw them put three wins on the board without conceding more than one goal in any of them. Rio Ngumoha opened the scoring after eight minutes — his second goal in three appearances since arriving from Liverpool — before Yacel Amrizi doubled the lead just before the break and finished the afternoon with a 7.8 rating, the best individual performance of the whole preseason run.

It's a fitting way for Amrizi to head into a Premier League season where his squad spot is anything but guaranteed. Signed as a free agent a year ago, he's spent the summer sharing a shirt number with a teenager the club paid eight figures for, and today he outplayed him. Whether that earns him minutes once the games start counting is a different question — but it's the kind of afternoon that makes benching him harder than it looked in June.

Two Months, One Chequebook, One Name We Still Can't Get Right

The board finally spent it. The goalkeeper we signed twice still didn't have a face until this week.

Nine weeks since we lifted the Championship trophy and I still haven't written anything down about what's happened since, which tells you something about how the summer's actually gone. Okonkwo's tricep, Ward wanting out and getting his wish, Dobson to Besiktas, Kaboré's loan running out and City not wanting him back permanently, Rodriguez walking on a free he'd already agreed with someone else before we'd even said goodbye properly. None of it dramatic on its own. All of it adding up to a squad that felt thinner in July than it did the day we won the league.

And then there's the goalkeeper. We signed a kid from Germany in June, called him Lenny Kuhn in every note I wrote, then got told that was wrong and it was actually Finn Richter, and now — this week — told that was wrong too. His name is Leo Bergmann. Sixteen years old, POT into the high eighties, and it took us three names and five weeks to get the one on his shirt correct. Earle's German network is going to produce good players. I'd like it, once, to produce a name we get right the first time.

Third time's the charm, apparently. I've stopped being embarrassed about it and started being amused. That's probably healthier.

— KJ

The actual news this week is better than any of that. We paid real money for the first time all summer — £8.7m for an eighteen-year-old Ajax midfielder, Jorthy Mokio, who can play three positions and looked like he belonged in every clip Earle sent over. And we picked up Toni Fruk on a free, twenty-five, and he's immediately our best forward on paper, which says as much about what we didn't have at striker as it does about him. Kieffer's decline was always going to force our hand eventually. Better it happens in July than in a relegation six-pointer in March.

Meanwhile the kids keep winning things without me having to think about them much, which is its own kind of luxury. Lord, Vargas and Soria have scored in every knockout match of this new tournament run, same as the last one. There's a French fourteen-year-old, Drouet, just arrived with a scouting report that reads like a lottery ticket. Premier League preseason starts soon and for the first time since May I feel like we're building toward it instead of just patching holes in it.

— KJ

Vargas Does It All as Wrexham's Kids Lift the Rush Tournament

A goal, two assists, and a trophy for a sixteen-year-old who has now had a better summer than half the first team.

Wrexham's academy side beat Blackburn 4–1 in Thursday's Youth Academy Rush Tournament final, and it was every bit as one-sided as the scoreline suggests — controlled from the first whistle, in the words of one member of the coaching staff who watched from the touchline with the look of someone already thinking about August. Nathaniel Matthews scored twice, Emiliano Bianchi got the third, and Juan Cruz Vargas capped it with a goal and two assists that earned him Man of the Match honours in the final of a tournament his side didn't lose a single match in.

It is, on paper, a youth trophy — the kind of result that would barely make the local paper at most Championship clubs, let alone a Premier League one. At Wrexham this summer, it has meant something closer to the opposite: a running audition, staged in public, for a handful of teenagers the club is increasingly convinced it doesn't need to sell.

You don't win a tournament like that by accident. You win it because the kid at the center of it already thinks he belongs on the other pitch.

— Academy staff, on Vargas

Whether that belief survives contact with a Premier League preseason is the question this club will spend August answering.

Five Out the Door, One Kid Sent Packing — and Somewhere in There, a Golden Goal

Jenkins clears the goalkeeping logjam and cashes in on last season's fringe players in the same afternoon Wrexham needed extra time to beat Ipswich's kids.

It was supposed to be a Youth Academy Rush Tournament fixture, the kind of thing that barely registers outside the training ground. Instead May 15th turned into the busiest day of Wrexham's summer so far. By the time Matthews' golden goal had settled a 2–1 extra-time win over Ipswich, the real news had already broken elsewhere: Danny Ward sold to Utrecht for $1.3m, Josh Windass to Wolves for $2.6m, Nathan Broadhead to Olympique Lyonnais for $4.5m, Zak Vyner to SC Braga for $2.4m, and Davis Keillor-Dunn to Levante for $2.55m — five first-team departures in a single afternoon, the biggest single-day clearout of the Jenkins era.

Ward's exit had been coming since he handed in a transfer request earlier in the summer, unhappy despite a completed development plan; sources close to the club say Jenkins was already leaning toward moving him on regardless, with Arthur Okonkwo returning from injury and seventeen-year-old Mason Webber next in line for minutes between the posts. The other four are harder to read as anything but a straightforward culling of the squad's fringes — useful last season, surplus to a Premier League one.

The academy had its own reckoning. Oliver Horne and Pieter Dijkgraaf both asked out, judged not yet ready for a first-team pathway and unwilling to wait. Anton Gassner didn't ask — he was shown the door, after deciding, apparently out loud and more than once, that he was already good enough for the senior squad. Wrexham's academy staff were unambiguous about the message that sends to the seventeen others still waiting their turn: no ultimatums. In his place, the club has brought in a new young goalkeeper, Lenny Kuhn, to restock the position.

You can want it. You can even be right that you want it. What you can't do is tell this club it owes you a shirt.

All of which made the actual football almost an afterthought. Lord and Matthews shared the goals, the second arriving deep into extra time to spare Wrexham's kids a penalty shootout. Nobody at the training ground seemed to be thinking about it by full time. There was too much else going on.
