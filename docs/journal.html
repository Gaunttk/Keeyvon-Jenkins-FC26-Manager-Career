October 18, 2025 · bet365 Stadium, Stoke-on-Trent

FOUR GOALS IN THIRTY MINUTES: STOKE EXPOSE WREXHAM'S DEFENSIVE FRAGILITY IN SOBERING AWAY ROUT

Kieffer Moore had Wrexham ahead at halftime. Then the second forty-five arrived, and so did the Stoke City that Jenkins's back line had never met before.

At the break — the bet365 at halftime, Stoke fans filing to the concessions, scoreboard reading 0-1 — Keeyvon Jenkins's team looked like the version that had beaten Leicester three weeks earlier. Compact. Organised. A goal up. Kieffer Moore had put them ahead at sixteen minutes with the kind of instinctive finish he delivers so quietly that crowds half-miss it, and the first forty-five came and went without Stoke threatening to change the picture. Wrexham went in ahead. That is worth stating plainly, because what followed requires stating just as plainly.

Sam Thomas equalised on 61 minutes. Lasse Cissé made it 2-1 on 74. Ashton Phillips added a third on 87. Melle Manhoef — on as a substitute since the 62nd minute — finished the rout in stoppage time. Four goals in thirty minutes of football. No response. Wrexham arrived at the Potteries looking like a team that might take three points and left on the wrong end of their heaviest defeat of the season.

The number that will follow this match into Wrexham's data room: beaten by opponent, 11. Stoke won eleven individual duels against a Wrexham player; Wrexham managed two in return. That is not a scoreline distorted by a goalkeeper's hot night or a lucky goal — it is a physical statement from a Stoke side sitting seventeenth in the table before kick-off, and it landed cleanly. Wrexham's tackle success rate — 33 per cent — confirms the picture. Dan Hyam's yellow card at 67 minutes forced a defensive substitution; Max Cleworth came on, the shape shifted, and Cissé scored seven minutes later. Whether the reshuffle invited the third and fourth goals is a conversation for the Wrexham training ground. The goals are a matter of record either way.

Arthur Okonkwo finished the evening rated 5.2, the lowest figure he has posted all season. Callum Doyle, at 7.2, was Wrexham's standout — a twenty-two-year-old centre-back holding the back line together in the half where everything around him unravelled. Moore's goal was his eighth of the Championship season. The individual numbers are good. The collective number — four conceded in a second half Wrexham managed to lead — is the one that matters this week.

"We had more passes than Stoke. We came away on the wrong end of four-one. You can make of that what you will."

— Wrexham supporter, post-match outside the bet365

In the background, timed with the kind of coincidence that the football calendar occasionally produces: confirmation on October 14th that Thomas O'Connor has agreed personal terms with Kocaelispor, a deal rated Excellent at $1.25 million, set to complete in the January window. O'Connor is a centre-back and defensive midfielder. Wrexham conceded four goals four days after his departure was signed and sealed. The decision to move him on was made in August. Saturday's second half is a separate matter. The timing, however, is not something any Wrexham supporter will easily miss.

The Night the Wheels Came Off

We led at half-time. We lost four-one. Both of those things are true.

I'll start with the part I can live with: the first forty-five minutes. Moore scored at sixteen, we looked tight at the back, we held the lead into halftime. I walked into that dressing room feeling genuinely okay. Not comfortable — you're never comfortable at Stoke in the Championship — but okay. We were in control.

The second half is something I need to write out and leave here, because I don't want to keep turning it over. Thomas equalised at sixty-one. Hyam got his yellow at sixty-seven — I had to bring Cleworth on, couldn't wait. Cissé scored seven minutes after that. Then Phillips. Then Manhoef in stoppage time. Four goals in thirty minutes of football, and we had no answer for a single one of them.

The "beaten by opponent" number was eleven to two. Eleven times they won a duel against one of my players. I don't have a tactical fix for that. It's physicality and mentality, and on Saturday we came up short in both.

Doyle was the best player on our side — 7.2, which on a night like that is impressive. He's twenty-two. He held his corner while everything around him fell apart, and I'll remember that. Okonkwo had a rough one. 5.2. He's twenty-four and he's shown me enough to know it won't define him, but it's on record and he knows it too.

The CEO confirmed O'Connor sold to Kocaelispor for $1.25 million on the drive back. Deal done in August, completes in January. I knew it was coming. The timing of the notification — while we were losing by three — is the kind of thing you can't script. I had to laugh. Thomas was on the transfer list because the decision made sense. Four goals conceded in one half doesn't change that. But I'll be honest: I noticed the timing.

We're still top of this league. Twenty-two points from ten games. Wherever that puts us in the table, it's at the summit, and we got there the hard way against sides that didn't give us anything. One bad half doesn't erase that. Next game. Erase it. Respond.

— KJ

October 4, 2025 · Racecourse Ground, Wrexham

RODRÍGUEZ BRACE STEADIES WREXHAM AFTER BIRMINGHAM LEVEL TWICE IN 4-2 THRILLER

Two goals up inside twenty-three minutes, level at the break, ahead again by the hour. The scoreline says routine. The middle forty-five said something else entirely.

Nathan Broadhead had it 1-0 inside twelve minutes, the kind of early strike that's supposed to settle a home crowd into an afternoon of formality. José Luis Rodríguez made it two before the half-hour, arrowing a finish past Birmingham's goalkeeper with the same unbothered ease he's shown since the day he walked in as a free-agent nobody had heard of. Two-nil, twenty minutes gone, and the Racecourse was doing the thing crowds do when a result feels decided early.

It was not decided. Kyle Fujimoto pulled one back on 29 minutes, and Charlie Klarer levelled it right on the stroke of half-time. Wrexham walked in at the break with the scoreline square, the possession count reading 57% in Birmingham's favour, and a home dressing room that had gone from cruising to defending in the space of fifteen minutes.

Rodríguez settled it for good on 63 minutes, his second of the afternoon and the goal that finally put Birmingham's momentum back in a drawer. Bailey Cadamarteri added a fourth in the 88th, the finish itself routine but the buildup anything but — Danny Keillor-Dunn's backheel through the Birmingham back line was the kind of pass that gets replayed regardless of the scoreline it's attached to. Rodríguez finished the day with 8.4, Player of the Match for the second matchday running, and a growing case that Wrexham's best summer business was a free transfer nobody outside Wrexham noticed.

"They had the ball more than us, more passes, more territory. Didn't matter. We had Rodríguez twice and they had two goals total. That's the whole match in one sentence."

— a Wrexham supporter outside the Racecourse, full time

Wrexham move to 22 points and a share of the division's better away-and-home records, still unbeaten anywhere but Millwall since August. But a manager who talks about control conceding two goals at home in fifteen first-half minutes is a data point worth holding onto. Ipswich and the sides above Wrexham don't offer Birmingham's generosity in front of goal. The thesis for this column has always been whether Jenkins's structure survives contact with the Championship's better teams. Saturday's fifteen minutes were the first real contact. It survived. Barely, and only because Rodríguez keeps turning up.

Ten Minutes I Didn't Enjoy

We won by two. For about a quarter of an hour, I wasn't sure we would.

Two goals up in twenty-three minutes and I was already thinking about who comes off at the hour. Then Fujimoto scored, then Klarer scored right before half-time, and I spent the interval saying things to this group I hadn't needed to say since Millwall. Tighten up. Stop switching off after we score. It's one thing to know it, another to feel your stomach drop watching it happen live from the touchline.

Rodríguez bailed us out again — second matchday running he's been the difference, and I'm running out of ways to describe what he does that don't sound like I'm exaggerating. He isn't. Two goals today, on top of the one at Leicester last week. A free transfer. I keep having to remind myself of that.

The goal I'll actually remember is Keillor-Dunn's backheel for Cadamarteri. No-look, first time, through three Birmingham players who had no idea it was coming. I didn't call that in training. Nobody could have. That's a player trusting his own instincts more than my instructions, and for once I'm glad he did.

Four goals covers a lot of sins. It doesn't erase the fifteen minutes where we forgot how to defend a lead.

Twenty-two points, seventh win of the season, and I'm proud of the response after halftime. But I noted the names on their scoresheet and I noted how easy it looked for them. Somewhere down this table there's a team that punishes that spell instead of only drawing level. I'd like to fix it before we find out who.

— KJ

September 28, 2025 · Leicester

LEICESTER DON'T LAND A SINGLE SHOT ON TARGET IN 3-0 COLLAPSE

Two shots. Zero on target. Wrexham didn't have to be spectacular to win by three — the home side simply never turned up in front of goal.

Leicester had the ball for most of the afternoon — 58% possession, 206 passes to Wrexham's 128 — and it amounted to nothing. Two shots in ninety-plus minutes, neither of them troubling Arthur Okonkwo's goal-line. Josh Windass opened the scoring on 31 minutes, Kieffer Moore doubled it early in the second half and picked up Player of the Match for his trouble, and José Luis Rodríguez rounded it off deep into stoppage time. Three different scorers, three different moments, and Leicester's back line watching all of them from the wrong side of the ball.

Moore's finish was his sixth Championship goal of the season, level with Jordan Ayew and one behind the division's early Golden Boot pace. It's the sort of number that doesn't generate headlines the way Rodríguez's arrival did, but it's the same number, quietly compiled, matchday after matchday. Two yellow cards for the home side — Danny Hyam and Ben Nelson, both inside the first ten minutes — set an early tone Leicester never recovered from. Wrexham made three substitutions after the hour and lost nothing in doing it, which says as much about the squad's depth as the scoreline says about its finishing.

Wrexham travel to the Championship's mid-table without much difficulty these days, and results like this one — comfortable, low-event, entirely controlled — are quietly becoming the norm rather than the exception. The question this column has asked all season is whether an American coach with American owners can win this league with a Welsh club playing this way. Nights like Leicester don't answer it. They just make the answer look less improbable than it did in August.

The Quiet Ones Count Too

We let them have the ball all day. We didn't need it.

Two shots against us all match. That's the number I keep coming back to. Not the three goals we scored, not Moore's finish, not Rodríguez rounding it off in stoppage time — two shots, zero on target, from a Championship side playing at home with 58% of the ball. That's discipline. That's a back line and a midfield doing their jobs quietly enough that nobody outside this room will ever mention it.

Moore's goal was his sixth of the league season. Nobody's writing columns about that the way they did about Rodríguez's first weeks here, and I understand why — a striker who scores becomes a habit, and habits don't make headlines. But I told him after the game that I notice it. Six goals, matchday after matchday, no fuss. That's exactly what I want a No. 9 to be.

After Millwall, I wasn't sure this group knew how to win a match like this — one where the other side has the ball and the pressure and we just have to be patient and clinical. Tonight we were both. Windass finishing well, Rathbone and Dobson doing a job off the bench, Amrizi closing it out. Nobody had to be a hero. That's a different kind of win than QPR or Derby, and honestly, I think I needed to see this one more than those.

The wins that don't feel dramatic are usually the ones that tell you the most about what a team has become.

Nineteen points from eight games. Clean sheet on the road. I'll take a boring night at Leicester over a chaotic one every time.

— KJ

KABORÉ'S HEADER AND OKONKWO'S CLEAN SHEET SINK DERBY 3-0

A right back who isn't supposed to score climbed above two centre-halves and nodded one past the far post. It was, without argument, the best goal the Racecourse has seen all season.

Keillor-Dunn opened it in the 13th minute, the kind of tidy finish that barely gets a second look in the highlights reel. Rodríguez doubled it on 26, calm as you like, and picked up an assist besides. Both were good goals. Neither is the one anyone left the Racecourse talking about.

That belonged to Ibrahim Kaboré. 54th minute, a scramble on the edge of the box, and Wrexham's right back — the one whose job description reads "defend, don't get forward too much" — rose above the Derby back line and buried a header near post. The away end went quiet. The home end did not.

The Header Nobody Called

Kaboré doesn't score. Tonight he did, and it was the best goal I've seen since I took this job.

I've watched Ibrahim Kaboré play right back for two months now. Reads the game well, tackles clean, gets forward when it's safe and recovers when it isn't. Not once did I picture him rising above two Derby centre-halves and heading one into the far corner. I don't think he pictured it either — the celebration told me that much.

Three goals, clean sheet, second in the table. Keillor-Dunn and Rodríguez did the professional work in the first half. Kaboré did the unforgettable work in the second. And Okonkwo — four saves, composed all night, walks away with the match ball in spirit even though he didn't score. That's the kind of thing I want this squad to notice about each other: the goalkeeper who keeps you level is worth as much as the header that wins it.

Some nights the story writes itself. You just have to be standing on the touchline when it happens.

Sixteen points from seven games. One point off the top. The gap in midfield I worried about in August hasn't reappeared — O'Brien and Rathbone covered well after the double sub. Derby had the ball more than us and it didn't matter, because we made our moments count and Okonkwo made sure theirs didn't.

I'll remember Kaboré's header longer than I'll remember the scoreline.

— KJ

WREXHAM SURVIVE A NORWICH SIEGE TO WIN 3-1 ON THE BACK OF TWO SUBSTITUTES

Norwich had two-thirds of the ball and nearly double the passes. Wrexham had Kieffer Moore's opener and two second-half substitutes who settled it.

Carrow Road is not an easy place to hold a lead, and for long spells on Saturday it looked like Wrexham wouldn't. Norwich had 66% of the possession, strung together 245 passes to Wrexham's 126, and generally played the match the away side is supposed to play at home. None of it showed up on the scoreboard.

Kieffer Moore opened the scoring in the 14th minute off a Noah Broadhead assist, and from there Wrexham did what counter-attacking sides do against possession-heavy hosts: they waited. Yacel Amrizi, on for Broadhead at 69', scored seven minutes later — Ibrahim Kaboré, overlapping from right back, laid it on. George Dobson, on at the same time for O'Brien, made it 3-1 in the 89th with an assist from Amrizi. Three goals, three different assists, one from a full back who wasn't supposed to be that far forward.

Wrexham's defending was patient rather than dominant, and it held anyway — helped by two substitutes who found the game's two biggest moments.

The thesis holds for another week: an American coach, an American ownership group, a Welsh club, winning ugly when it needs to.

Won Away, Won Ugly, Won

We had a third of the ball all afternoon. I'll take three points over possession every single time.

Sixty-six percent possession for Norwich. Nearly double our pass count. If you'd shown me those numbers at halftime without the score, I'd have assumed we were hanging on for a draw at best. Instead we're flying back to Wrexham with three points and a goal difference I'm proud of.

Moore's opener in the 14th settled everyone down early. Then it was a long, patient wait — soaking up Norwich's pressure, trusting the back line, trusting Okonkwo behind them. I made the double change at 69' — Amrizi and Dobson for Broadhead and O'Brien — mostly for legs, a little for spark. Got both. Amrizi scored seven minutes after coming on. Dobson finished it in the 89th, assisted by the same Amrizi who'd just scored. That's the kind of impact I want from the bench, and getting it twice in one afternoon isn't luck — it's depth doing its job.

You don't have to dominate a match to win it. You have to be better in the moments that count. Today we were.

The concerning part, if I'm honest with this page the way I try to be: Norwich should have made more of that possession. Better sides will. We got away with patient defending today; we won't always. But three points is three points, and second place with a game like this behind us says something about the character in this squad.

— KJ

RODRIGUEZ ANNOUNCES HIMSELF WITH A DEBUT BRACE AS WREXHAM DISMANTLE QPR 4-0

The free transfer nobody talked about scored twice in the first half, assisted a third, and subbed off at 70 minutes with the game long over. Welcome to Wrexham.

There is a particular cruelty to good timing. Eight days after the Millwall defeat — a night that left Jenkins searching for words and Wrexham searching for identity — José Luis Rodríguez walked onto the Racecourse pitch for the first time and made the question look simple.

23rd minute: a Finesse Shot curled past the QPR keeper from the edge of the area. 29th minute: he did it again. 35th minute: his pass found Kieffer Moore, who made it three before half-time. By the time Rodríguez was pulled at 70 minutes — game finished, point made — the Racecourse was already trying to figure out where he'd been hiding.

He'd been hiding in free agency. Signed on the last day of the window, no fee, no announcement beyond a cursory line in the club's social media. The kind of signing that gets filed under depth and forgotten until it isn't.

He Came In Quiet

I called him a free agent nobody was talking about. Eight days later he scored twice on debut. The window looks different now.

I wrote in this journal eight days ago that Rodriguez "comes in quiet." That I wasn't sure we were creating enough chances for anyone to finish them anyway. I stand by the second part — that problem is real and will come back. But the first part I got wrong.

He did not come in quiet. He came in to a standing ovation he doesn't know he's earned yet. 23rd minute, 29th minute, assist for the third. Subbed at 70 because the game was over and there was no point risking his legs. The Racecourse was doing things I haven't heard it do in my short time here.

I talked to Thomason earlier this week. He pushed to play — said he was in form, said he deserved minutes. I told him I hadn't worked out the team yet, that I'd bear it in mind. That's manager-speak for "I hear you but I'm not promising anything." Turns out he was right. He started. Played a 7.0. Kept his side of the street clean. That's the kind of conversation that gets easier when the player backs it up, and harder when they don't. Today it got easier.

You don't know who a player is until the league starts pressing back. Rodriguez just got pressed back and smiled.

Second in the table. Ten points from five games. Broadhead leading the division in assists. Moore and Keillor-Dunn both on four goals. And now Rodriguez. If someone had told me after The Den that this week would look like this, I'd have thought they were trying to make me feel better.

The gap in midfield is still real. The defensive question will come up again. September has more to say. But for one afternoon at the Racecourse, everything worked the way it's supposed to work. The team was controlled from start to finish. QPR had more possession and generated nothing. We had less of the ball and scored four.

He came in quiet. And then he spoke.

— KJ

WREXHAM SWALLOWED WHOLE AT THE DEN

Millwall 2-0 Wrexham. The Championship served Jenkins his first proper reality check.

There is a particular kind of silence that settles over a visiting dugout when the home crowd senses blood. By the hour mark at The Den on Saturday, that silence had found Keeyvon Jenkins.

Millwall were not spectacular. They didn't need to be. They were organised, physical, and relentless in the press — everything Wrexham have aspired to be — and they executed it better. Fisayo Azeez broke the deadlock in the 33rd minute and picked up a yellow card ten minutes later for the trouble he caused afterward; Marko Ivanović added a second in stoppage time to make the scoreline match the performance. The Red Dragons, who had looked so assured in the opening fortnight, could not fashion a single clean chance — one shot on target from four attempts. The ball came back to them like a question they couldn't answer.

Jenkins spoke afterward about "identity" and "the process." The kind of language that sounds reassuring in preseason and hollow after a 2-0 on a muddy pitch in south London. Jenkins Arrives. The Clock Is Already Ticking.

The American takes the Wrexham job with the club rooted to the bottom of the Championship and a board demanding the impossible.

There was something almost cinematic about it — which, given who owns this football club, felt entirely appropriate.

Keeyvon Jenkins walked into his Wrexham AFC unveiling on June 30th in a grey V-neck and red tie, looking less like a man inheriting a crisis and more like someone who'd been planning this moment for years. The former USMNT midfielder, 38 years old and carrying the kind of quiet confidence that only comes from having played at the highest level, sat behind the microphones at STōK Cae Ras and said all the right things.

The official club account posted the press conference clip within the hour. 1.98K likes before sundown.

What the post didn't mention — what nobody wanted to say out loud on day one — is that Wrexham are 24th in the EFL Championship. Bottom. The Hollywood dream that began in the National League, that carried this club through three consecutive promotions and a documentary series watched by millions, has run headfirst into the reality of English football's second tier.

Reynolds and McElhenney have made bold calls before. Appointing an American manager with no top-flight European head coaching experience is either the boldest yet — or the most desperate.

"I fell in love with Wales during my Swansea years. When Ryan and Rob called, I didn't think twice. This club has unfinished business."

The board's mandate is unambiguous: automatic promotion. Not a playoff push. Not consolidation. Up. This season. Jenkins inherits a dressing room that needs belief more than tactics right now. Whether "The Hawk" — as American soccer fans have called him since his Fulham days — can deliver that in time remains the only question that matters in North Wales this summer.
