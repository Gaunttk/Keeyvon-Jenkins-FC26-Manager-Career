RODRIGUEZ ANNOUNCES HIMSELF WITH A DEBUT BRACE AS WREXHAM DISMANTLE QPR 4-0

The free transfer nobody talked about scored twice in the first half, assisted a third, and subbed off at 70 minutes with the game long over. Welcome to Wrexham.

There is a particular cruelty to good timing. Eight days after the Millwall defeat — a night that left Jenkins searching for words and Wrexham searching for identity — José Luis Rodríguez walked onto the Racecourse pitch for the first time and made the question look simple.

23rd minute: a Finesse Shot curled past the QPR keeper from the edge of the area. 29th minute: he did it again. 35th minute: his pass found Kieffer Moore, who made it three before half-time. By the time Rodríguez was pulled at 70 minutes — game finished, point made — the Racecourse was already trying to figure out where he'd been hiding.

He'd been hiding in free agency. Signed on the last day of the window, no fee, no announcement beyond a cursory line in the club's social media. The kind of signing that gets filed under depth and forgotten until it isn't.

The numbers were convincing in every direction. Wrexham had 11 shots to QPR's four, a 94% dribble success rate, 91% shot accuracy, and an xG gap that rarely lies. QPR's pass accuracy was superior — 87% to 81% — but that is the kind of statistic that wins arguments and loses games. They had the ball going nowhere while Wrexham turned their limited possession into four goals.

Davis Keillor-Dunn came off the bench at 62 minutes and scored within six. George Thomason, who'd lobbied Jenkins for minutes this week, started and delivered a composed 7.0 at left back. Lewis O'Brien collected two assists from deep. Arthur Okonkwo faced one shot on target and saved it. Clean sheet, first half over, Racecourse singing.

"Nine-point-six. In a debut. Against a Championship side. For free."

— Post-match WhatsApp from a scout who'd passed on Rodríguez three months ago

Wrexham sit second, two points behind Ipswich. Broadhead leads the division in assists with four. Moore and Keillor-Dunn are joint-fifth in goals. The window that looked like it closed dirty — James sold, Burton sold, one quiet free agent in — looks considerably smarter this morning.

Owen Meredith's thesis for the season was whether an American coach could win an English league with a Welsh club. After watching Rodríguez walk in from nowhere and rearrange the narrative in 70 minutes: the question remains open, but it just got more interesting.

He Came In Quiet

I called him a free agent nobody was talking about. Eight days later he scored twice on debut. The window looks different now.

I wrote in this journal eight days ago that Rodriguez "comes in quiet." That I wasn't sure we were creating enough chances for anyone to finish them anyway. I stand by the second part — that problem is real and will come back. But the first part I got wrong.

He did not come in quiet. He came in to a standing ovation he doesn't know he's earned yet. 23rd minute, 29th minute, assist for the third. Subbed at 70 because the game was over and there was no point risking his legs. The Racecourse was doing things I haven't heard it do in my short time here.

I talked to Thomason earlier this week. He pushed to play — said he was in form, said he deserved minutes. I told him I hadn't worked out the team yet, that I'd bear it in mind. That's manager-speak for "I hear you but I'm not promising anything." Turns out he was right. He started. Played a 7.0. Kept his side of the street clean. That's the kind of conversation that gets easier when the player backs it up, and harder when they don't. Today it got easier.

You don't know who a player is until the league starts pressing back. Rodriguez just got pressed back and smiled.

Second in the table. Ten points from five games. Broadhead leading the division in assists. Moore and Keillor-Dunn both on four goals. And now Rodriguez. If someone had told me after The Den that this week would look like this, I'd have thought they were trying to make me feel better.

The gap in midfield is still real. The defensive question will come up again. September has more to say. But for one afternoon at the Racecourse, everything worked the way it's supposed to work. The team was controlled from start to finish. QPR had more possession and generated nothing. We had less of the ball and scored four.

He came in quiet. And then he spoke.

— KJ

WREXHAM SWALLOWED WHOLE AT THE DEN

Millwall 2-0 Wrexham. The Championship served Jenkins his first proper reality check.

There is a particular kind of silence that settles over a visiting dugout when the home crowd senses blood. By the hour mark at The Den on Saturday, that silence had found Keeyvon Jenkins.

Millwall were not spectacular. They didn't need to be. They were organised, physical, and relentless in the press — everything Wrexham have aspired to be — and they executed it better. The Red Dragons, who had looked so assured in the opening fortnight, could not fashion a single clean chance. The ball came back to them like a question they couldn't answer.

Season Record W2 D1 L1 · 7 pts

Jenkins spoke afterward about "identity" and "the process." The kind of language that sounds reassuring in preseason and hollow after a 2-0 on a muddy pitch in south London. The question isn't whether Wrexham can play football. They've shown they can. The question is whether they can play it when the opposition decides they won't be allowed to.

"We didn't show up. There's no other way to say it."

— Jenkins, post-match presser

The window closes Monday. James is gone to Middlesbrough. Burton sold abroad. A free agent striker arrives with no fanfare. It feels, on a grey August evening in SE16, like a squad that shed more than it gained.

The Window Closes Dirty

Sold two. Bought one. Lost badly. The summer ends on its worst note.

I've been sitting with the Millwall tape for two days now and I keep arriving at the same place: we didn't compete. Not tactically — I mean the basic stuff. The duels. The second balls. The willingness to be ugly when ugly is what's required.

Millwall didn't beat us with ideas. They beat us with intent. And that's the part that stings, because intent is supposed to be non-negotiable.

The window has officially closed. Burton sold to Hermannstadt — fair price, right situation, no hard feelings. Matty James to Middlesbrough. That one I'll second-guess. He was declining, yes, but he was experienced, and the midfield is thinner now. Ashfield is still on loan. Dijkgraaf is seventeen and in the academy. The gap is real.

The gap in the middle of the park is not a future problem. It's a now problem.

The new striker — Rodriguez — comes in quiet. Free agent. No fanfare. We'll see what he is. Right now I'm not sure we're creating enough chances for anyone to finish them anyway.

Four games in. Seven points. The board wants promotion. I want to look less like a team playing its first season together and more like a unit. September will tell us a lot about which one we actually are.

— KJ

Blackburn End Wrexham's Cup Run in a Night to Forget at the Racecourse

A single goal was enough. Wrexham generated almost nothing going forward, and Keeyvon Jenkins's side exit the Carabao Cup at Round 2 without a real argument.

There are nights when a team simply doesn't function — when the shape is there on paper and nothing translates to the pitch. Wednesday at the Racecourse was one of those nights. Blackburn Rovers came, scored once, and defended the rest. Wrexham had one shot. One.

It is the kind of result that is difficult to dress up. Jenkins's side have been exceptional going forward in the Championship — Keillor-Dunn and Moore combining for seven goals in three league games — but the Cup brought a different energy, and Wrexham had none of it. Blackburn sat deep, closed the space, and waited. Wrexham never found a way through.

The one consolation — if it can be called that — was Issouf Kaboré, named Man of the Match in a game Wrexham lost. Which tells you most of what you need to know about how the rest of the side performed. Kaboré has been the engine of this team's press since August began. He was the engine again here. He just had nothing to push forward.

One Shot. One Goal Against. A Mess.

Some nights there is no tactical explanation. You just didn't show up.

I'm not going to sit here and write a thoughtful analysis. We had one shot. One. Against a side we should be able to break down at home, in our own stadium, in front of our own fans.

One In, One Out: Wrexham's Quiet Business Continues

Yacel Amrizi arrived on a free and already has a Championship cameo to his name. Thomas O'Connor wants a fresh start — and the club isn't standing in his way.

No unveiling, no scarf photo, nothing for the highlight reel. Yacel Amrizi simply arrived, signed, and two days later was warming up on the touchline at the Racecourse. That's how the smart business gets done in this division — not with fanfare, but with paperwork and a manager who already knows where the gap in his squad is.

Amrizi, 21, joins on a free transfer from his previous club, a five-year deal on the books and a market value the recruitment team have pegged at £6.5m — the kind of arithmetic that makes a director of football smile. He made his Wrexham debut off the bench in the 1–1 draw with Sheffield Wednesday, replacing Broadhead on the left with twenty-odd minutes to chase a winner that never came. Nothing sensational. A tidy 6.4 and a place in the squad photo. Early days.

Going the other way, in spirit if not yet on paper: Thomas O'Connor. The centre-back has been placed on the transfer list, and by his own account it's a decision he's relieved to see made. "I haven't really enjoyed playing here," he told the club, "and I think a move's probably in everyone's best interests." No histrionics, no farewell tour — just two professionals agreeing the fit had run its course.

Wrexham's summer business has been defined less by statement signings than by margins — a free here, a sensible exit there, a squad quietly reshaping itself around a front line that's already delivering. Amrizi is a bet on upside at zero cost. O'Connor's departure, whenever it's finalised, tidies up a squad that no longer has room for players who'd rather be elsewhere.

Margins

You don't win promotions with the signings that make headlines. You win them with the ones nobody notices.

Got Amrizi in for nothing. Twenty-one, sharp movement, a shooting number that made me sit up when the scouts flagged him. Free transfer — the finance tip on the deal screen actually said "bargain," which is about as close to a compliment as this game's front office gets. Threw him on for the last twenty at Sheffield Wednesday just to get a look. Didn't do anything wrong. Didn't do anything spectacular either. That's fine. Nobody's asking him to be Moore in week one.

O'Connor's situation resolved itself, more or less. Had the conversation about the transfer list expecting some pushback and got none — he'd already made his peace with it before I had. "Glad you've decided," he said, like I'd done him a favour. Maybe I had. He's a good pro, but good pros who've mentally checked out don't help you in December when the pitches turn heavy and you need bodies who want to be there.

Every window is two windows: the one you announce and the one that's just addition by subtraction.

Thinner at centre-back now with Scarr gone and O'Connor on his way out. I know it. The board knows it. Something will have to give before the window shuts — but I'd rather go into September light at the back with players who want to fight for the shirt than heavy with ones who don't.

Backs to the Wall: Wrexham Drop First Points at the Racecourse

Sheaf was immense, Keillor-Dunn equalised, but Jenkins's side still couldn't hold on. The Championship isn't the Carabao Cup.

The noise inside the Racecourse Ground told you before the final whistle confirmed it. This wasn't the composed, almost clinical performance that had dispatched Preston on Tuesday. This was the Championship doing what the Championship does — squeezing, pressing, refusing to be impressed.

Sheffield Wednesday arrived in Wrexham having watched the West Brom highlights. They had a plan. And for long stretches of this 1–1 draw, it worked.

The story of the afternoon, oddly, belonged to Wrexham's own B. Sheaf, who produced the individual performance of the day and still only walked off with a point to show for it. He was everywhere — shielding, pressing, winning second balls, dragging a stuttering midfield through ninety minutes more or less on his own. It is a strange kind of afternoon when your best player on the pitch wins the official Man of the Match award and the dressing room still isn't smiling.

Wrexham's goal came through D. Keillor-Dunn, who has started this Championship season like a man with a point to prove. His equaliser — his third goal in three matches, if you count the Carabao — brought the home crowd to life. For a few minutes, it felt like the Racecourse might roar its way to all three points.

It didn't. Wednesday's defensive block held, the draw stood, and Wrexham were left with a point that felt more like a dropped two — Sheaf's brilliance notwithstanding.

Two games into the Championship. Seven points available. Four collected. Wrexham sit in a position most sides would accept at this stage — but the manner of today's draw will linger. There's a difference between being pushed and being dominated. Today it was closer to the latter, and it took the best player on the pitch wearing your own crest to paper over it.

A draw that stings. Not because of the result — because of how it felt.

Some games you lose and still feel like you're building something. This wasn't one of those games.

Wednesday sat deep and compact and let us have the ball, daring us to break them down. We couldn't get Broadhead on the ball in the half-spaces all afternoon. Moore and Broadhead Dismantle the Baggies: Wrexham Make It Two From Two

West Brom arrived as one of the Championship's promotion favourites. They left having been picked apart by a Wrexham front line that is starting to look genuinely dangerous.

Callum Styles was the best player on the pitch. He'll want his teammates to remember that, because nobody else on the West Brom side came close to his level — and Wrexham still won 3–1.

That tells you something about how good Jenkins's side were here. Styles, the man Albion built their press around, their engine, their creative fulcrum — Man of the Match, unambiguously. And it wasn't enough. On another day, with a slightly different result in a slightly different game, Styles walks off having led his team to a point in hostile territory. Today, he walked off while Wrexham fans sang in the stands.

K. Moore opened the scoring with the kind of centre-forward movement that Championship defenders hate — dropping deep, spinning his marker, driving into the box. Then came N. Broadhead, twice. The first was clinical. The second was confident. By the time it was 3–1, it had stopped feeling like a contest.

Comfortable

First home Championship win. West Brom. It should have felt bigger.

When you win 3–1 at home against one of the pre-season favourites, you're supposed to feel a certain way. I felt good. I felt relieved. I didn't feel satisfied, and I'm still thinking about why that is.

The Carabao Awakening: Wrexham Stroll Into Round Two

Three days after a 4-1 statement at Southampton, Jenkins's side travelled to Preston and barely broke a sweat. The cup is no longer an afterthought at Wrexham.

Before we get to the result — and the result was good — it's worth stepping back and asking a question Wrexham supporters haven't had reason to ask in a long time: what even is this competition to them?

The Carabao Cup has changed its name eleven times since its 1960 founding. The final is still at Wembley. The big clubs still rotate. But for a side like Wrexham — one year into the Championship, still learning what this division costs — it is something else entirely. It is a Tuesday night on the road with a full squad, against opposition that doesn't have your scouting report yet. It is, in other words, a gift.

Keeyvon Jenkins used it like one.

His side travelled to Deepdale three days after dismantling Southampton and played with a composure that will have surprised the home faithful. Not reckless pressing, not desperate defending — structure. Wrexham looked like a team that had been coached. Nineteen minutes in, D. Keillor-Dunn — fresh off his Man of the Match showing on the opening day — converted to make it 1–0. Sixteen minutes later, J. Windass doubled the lead before half-time, and the second half became a rotation exercise. Jenkins rang the changes — Longman on for Broadhead, Rathbone on for Windass, Dobson on for Sheaf — and Preston couldn't find a way back in.

The number that caught my eye wasn't on the scoresheet. It was the expected goals figure: 3.6 for Wrexham against 0.6 for Preston. That is not a flattering margin. That is dominance wearing a modest scoreline. Wrexham created chances they didn't take — the sort of wastefulness that won't be punished in a cup tie but will be against Championship sides with something to prove.

"The Carabao Cup is where you learn who actually wants to play for you."

— Owen Meredith, on the fringe players' auditions at Deepdale

Ismaël Kaboré was the most interesting story of the night. The Burkina Faso international — listed as a right back, deployed as a right back, but clearly itching to get forward — picked up an assist and a 7.4 rating. Buried inside the stat sheet: 16 interceptions from the right back position, anchoring Wrexham's defensive shape while the attack did its work. Jenkins's system rewards the right back's engine. Kaboré might be the engine.

Wrexham have now won six consecutive fixtures across pre-season and competitive play. Round Two awaits.

First Blood

The cup competition nobody respects. We treated it like it mattered. That's the point.

Three days after Southampton, we went to Deepdale and did it again. Different competition, same result: composure, control, and a clean sheet. I circled August 12 in my notebook before kickoff — first Carabao Cup tie in charge. These are the moments that add up.

Dane got us going at nineteen minutes and I felt the bench exhale. Josh doubled it before half and we went in 2–0 up away from home in a proper cup tie. I rotated in the second half — Longman, Rathbone, Dobson all got minutes — and none of them let us down. That depth is real. Last season it wasn't. That's the difference.

Ismaël was the real story, though. Sixteen interceptions from right back. The man reads the game before it happens — he was always one step ahead of Preston's movement, cutting off angles before they became options. And then he's got the assist on top of that. I've been watching him carefully since pre-season and tonight confirmed what I suspected: if he can maintain that reading at Championship pace, we have something genuinely special in that position.

"Win the cup game. Then win the next one. The season isn't a story yet — it's just a Tuesday night in Preston."

The xG split tells the truth: 3.6 to 0.6. We left goals out there that we shouldn't have, and I've already flagged that with the forwards in the debrief. But the structure held. The press worked. We didn't concede a single shot on target. Arthur barely had a save to make.

Six wins from six across pre-season and competition. That number will stop mattering the moment we drop one. I know that. But tonight, in the dressing room at Deepdale, there was a quiet confidence I haven't felt yet from this group. Not arrogance. Belief. That's what you're building toward.

Round Two. Then Matchday 2. Then we keep going.

— KJ

Wrexham Announce Themselves With a 4-1 Statement on the South Coast

Keillor-Dunn and Moore both score twice, Broadhead pulls every string — but the same defensive lapses that haunted last season showed up again, even in victory.

Nobody in the away end at St Mary's expected this. Not 4-1. Not against a Southampton side many had pencilled in as automatic promotion contenders. But here was Wrexham, twenty minutes from kickoff into a new Championship campaign, playing like a club with something to prove.

Danny Keillor-Dunn was the headline. Two goals, an assist, Man of the Match in a performance that suggested last season's frustrations behind him. Kasey Moore matched him goal for goal. And in behind both of them, Noah Broadhead was the architect — three assists, pulling Southampton's back line apart with a vision that the highlight reel will not do justice to.

It wasn't flawless. Jenkins's press forced turnover after turnover in the final third, but there were stretches — particularly either side of the hour mark — where Southampton walked through a back line that looked unsure of its shape. A 4-1 scoreline flatters the gaps that were there to be exploited. Wrexham made far more of their chances than their opponents did, and on another day this is a different kind of result.

Off the pitch, the business has already started. Ryan Barnett left for Watford in a £1.3m deal — a player who'd given the club steady service on the right flank, but one Jenkins clearly felt he could do without given the depth he's building behind him. Danny Ward and Dan Scarr have both gone up for sale, with Aaron James shipped out on a loan listing. The exits suggest a manager trimming fat before he adds muscle.

I'll enjoy this win tonight. Tomorrow I'm back on the defensive shape, because the next team we get sloppy against won't be as generous as we were lucky.

The business side was harder than the match, honestly. Letting Barnett go to Watford for £1.3m — tough call. Preston in the cup next. Different test, different rhythm. But for tonight — 4-1 on the south coast. I'll sleep alright.

Three assists. One goal. Keeyvon Moore, the club's wide forward, was everywhere against Ceuta on July 22nd, operating in the pockets between lines in a way that made the Spanish side look like they'd never prepared for anything. Keillor-Dunn converted twice from his service. Cadamarteri and O'Brien got on the scoresheet. Moore took the Man of the Match award and looked faintly bored by the whole thing.

Four days earlier, Wrexham had dismissed Bradford City 4-0 in the European International Cup — Broadhead with two goals, Moore again involved, the system clicking with a fluency you rarely see before a ball has been kicked in anger. Jenkins watched both performances with the arms-folded calm of a man who is trying very hard not to look pleased with himself.

Jenkins Arrives. The Clock Is Already Ticking.

The American takes the Wrexham job with the club rooted to the bottom of the Championship and a board demanding the impossible.

There was something almost cinematic about it — which, given who owns this football club, felt entirely appropriate.

Keeyvon Jenkins walked into his Wrexham AFC unveiling on June 30th in a grey V-neck and red tie, looking less like a man inheriting a crisis and more like someone who'd been planning this moment for years. The former USMNT midfielder, 38 years old and carrying the kind of quiet confidence that only comes from having played at the highest level, sat behind the microphones at STōK Cae Ras and said all the right things.

The official club account posted the press conference clip within the hour. 1.98K likes before sundown.

What the post didn't mention — what nobody wanted to say out loud on day one — is that Wrexham are 24th in the EFL Championship. Bottom. The Hollywood dream that began in the National League, that carried this club through three consecutive promotions and a documentary series watched by millions, has run headfirst into the reality of English football's second tier.

Reynolds and McElhenney have made bold calls before. Appointing an American manager with no top-flight European head coaching experience is either the boldest yet — or the most desperate.
