Milán Vitális's Rare Start Delivers a Point as Wrexham and Arsenal Share the Spoils at the Racecourse

The teamsheet went up an hour before kickoff and the loudest reaction in the press box wasn't about Arsenal's front line. It was one name in Wrexham's midfield that hardly anyone had seen there since August.

Milán Vitális has made twenty-four appearances for this club since arriving from Slovakia and started, by Owen Meredith's count, fewer than a handful of them in the Premier League. He is the sort of player a squad accumulates rather than builds around — versatile enough to cover two positions, experienced enough not to panic, never quite spectacular enough to force his way past the names Jenkins reaches for first. On Saturday, with Bobadilla managed for fitness and Fruk carrying a knock from training, Vitális got the nod in central midfield against a title-chasing Arsenal side, and turned in the kind of ninety minutes that makes a manager's rotation policy look less like an accident and more like a plan. Man of the Match, by a distance nobody in the press box seriously disputed.

The goal itself belonged to someone with a more familiar name on the scoresheet. Yacel Amrizi rose to head in a Vitális-carried move in the 37th minute, his sixth Premier League goal of the season, and for a half hour either side of half-time the Racecourse allowed itself to believe this could be a statement result against one of the two clubs above them. Arsenal had other ideas. Kepa Barcola equalized four minutes into the second half, ghosting between Doyle and Heaven at the back post from a corner that Wrexham's back line will want back — the one genuine lapse in an otherwise composed defensive performance that saw both centre-backs rated 7.2. "We didn't lose anything today, and there were stretches where we were the better side against genuinely good opposition," Jenkins said afterward, careful with his framing. "But I'd be lying if I said a point at home to Arsenal doesn't sting a little when we had the game in our hands for twenty minutes."

There is a version of this result that reads as a missed opportunity, and Meredith heard plenty of that version from supporters filing out of the ground — a home game against a side directly above them in the table, a two-goal cushion within touching distance, and a point instead of three. But there is another version that says more about the squad Jenkins has quietly assembled underneath the marquee names: a player who spends most Saturdays on the bench can walk into a match of this magnitude and not look out of place. "Depth is the boring word for it, but that's what won us a promotion and it's what's kept us in this table's top four all autumn," one long-time Racecourse regular told Meredith, unprompted, on the walk back into town. Vitális's afternoon is exactly the kind of evidence that argument needs — and exactly the kind of story the club's academy-first recruitment model, still relatively new to the Premier League stage, will want repeated more than once.

Man City's win elsewhere on Saturday means Wrexham slip from third to fourth, still four points off top spot but now with Arsenal, City and United all sat above them heading into the final third of the calendar year. Next up is a trip to Nottingham Forest on November 28th — a side mid-table and unpredictable, the sort of fixture Jenkins has occasionally found trickier than the ones against the division's giants. Whether Saturday's midfield gamble becomes a one-off or the first sign of a squad Jenkins trusts more broadly than his teamsheet has suggested all season is the question Meredith intends to keep asking.

The Ones Who Don't Start

Five matchdays since the last one of these, and the stretch has been the best five-game run of my time here — with today's team sheet the part I keep coming back to.

I wrote last time about a staircase — up, up, up, and then a stumble. Since then it's been almost all staircase. We went to Selhurst Park and put four past Palace. We ground out a one-goal win at home to Villa that wasn't pretty but was exactly the kind of professional performance I've been asking for since preseason. And then Anfield happened, and Leo Sauer decided he was done being a squad player everyone described as "promising." Three wins in a row against a run of fixtures that could easily have gone the other way, and in the middle of it we sold Lewis O'Brien and pushed Juan Cruz Vargas into first-team training the same week — the kind of squad churn that usually costs you a result or two while everyone adjusts. It didn't cost us anything. I'm proud of that more than I've said out loud to the group.

Today was the outlier, and it's the one I want to write about, because a draw at home to Arsenal isn't really the story. Milán Vitális is. I picked him today mostly out of necessity — Bobadilla needed managing, Fruk knocked up in training — and he gave me ninety minutes that made me look twice at how I've been using him. Twenty-four appearances for this club and I could probably count his Premier League starts on one hand before today. That's on me as much as it's a compliment to him. I built a lot of this squad around potential — kids I signed for what they could become rather than what they already were. Vitális is the opposite bet: a finished player, twenty-four years old, who does his job quietly enough that it's easy to forget he's there until you need him and he delivers a Man of the Match performance against a title contender. I don't have a tidy answer yet for how much that should change my selection thinking. But it should change it some.

Depth isn't just insurance against injuries. Today it was the best player on the pitch. I need to stop treating players like Vitális as the ones who don't start and start treating them as the ones I haven't started enough.

— KJ

Leo Sauer's Brace at Anfield Sends Wrexham Third as Liverpool Are Swept Aside 3–0

Twenty minutes before kickoff, the Kop was still doing what the Kop does — scarves up, "You'll Never Walk Alone" rolling down from the stands like weather. By full time it had gone quiet enough to hear the away end sing every one of Leo Sauer's name three times over.

It didn't intimidate this one. Wrexham had lost the toss for a slow start at three of their last four away Premier League grounds; here, they simply didn't allow themselves one. Leo Sauer scored twice inside six second-half minutes — the 37th and the 43rd, either side of the half-time whistle in all but the literal sense — and Chido Obi added a stoppage-time third that turned a routine result into an emphatic one. By the time the final whistle went, sections of the Kop had already started filing for the exits, an image Owen Meredith's press-box neighbor from a Merseyside paper called "the loudest silence I've heard at this ground in a decade."

Sauer's performance — two goals, an assist, an 8.3 rating, a Man of the Match award that wasn't seriously contested by anyone else on the pitch — was the kind that changes how a player is talked about inside a building. Twenty years old, Welsh-eligible through a grandparent nobody at the club had bothered to mention publicly until this week, and until Saturday still filed under "promising" more often than "arrived." That filing cabinet needs relabeling now. "I've been telling you for a month that Leo was close to a performance like this," Jenkins said afterward, allowing himself something close to a grin in a press conference he otherwise kept businesslike. "Today he didn't just get close. He got there and then some."

There is a version of this club's story that Meredith has been circling in this column since the Hollywood ownership first arrived: the tension between a Wrexham that belongs to a town in north Wales and a Wrexham that now belongs, in some diffuse and slightly uncomfortable sense, to the world. An away win at Anfield, beamed out to whichever country still has FC26 open on a laptop past midnight, is exactly the kind of result that widens that gap further. A woman selling programmes outside the away end put it more plainly than any boardroom strategy document could: "Ten years ago nobody outside this town knew who we were. Now my nephew in Ohio texts me the score before I've even sat down." Inside the away end itself, the noise told its own story — a section that used to be filled almost entirely with red-and-white season-ticket regulars from the Racecourse now carries a visible scattering of American accents and Stateside-bought replica shirts, the strange, slightly surreal residue of a documentary that turned a fifth-tier club into a global object of curiosity. "I flew in from Austin for this one," one supporter, wrapped in a Wrexham scarf bought online rather than at the club shop, told Meredith outside the away end. "Never been to Wales in my life. Feels mad saying that out loud at Anfield."

None of that changes what actually happened on the pitch, and Jenkins was careful in his press conference to keep the two stories separate. But the club's growth off the pitch — the global audience, the reach into the United States, the way a result like this one gets discussed on message boards from Cardiff to California within minutes of the final whistle — is now inseparable from what happens on it, whether anyone at the Racecourse is entirely comfortable with that or not. Whether it stays a source of pride for a proud Welsh institution or eventually curdles into something more complicated is a conversation this column intends to keep having.

The other story from Anfield broke before kickoff rather than during it. Word reached the press box shortly before the teamsheets were confirmed that Wrexham had completed the sale of Lewis O'Brien to Fulham for a reported £5.9m, ending a spell in which the 27-year-old midfielder had drifted from regular starter to squad depth. More striking still was the news that followed it: Juan Cruz Vargas, the 17-year-old Argentine CAM long regarded as the academy's crown jewel, has been promoted to the first team with immediate effect. "This kid has the potential to be special," Jenkins told reporters, asked about the timing of a promotion that lands the same week as an away trip to Anfield. "I want him getting minutes with the first team now, not in six months when it's convenient." Vargas didn't feature at Anfield — a debut this soon would have been a stretch even for a manager as willing to gamble on youth as Jenkins has proven — but the message sent by promoting the club's highest-rated prospect on the same day as a marquee away fixture was not subtle, and it will not be lost on the rest of the academy intake watching from Wrexham.

Three points at Anfield, a Man of the Match performance from a player who spent most of September fighting for a starting shirt, and a teenager with a 90–94 potential ceiling now training with the first team — Meredith's column has had thinner weeks to write about. What comes next, with Arsenal at the Racecourse in a fortnight and a genuinely crowded top four still to be sorted out, is where this stretch of results gets properly tested.

Yacel Amrizi's Second-Half Strike Is Enough as Wrexham Grind Past Aston Villa

For sixty-eight minutes the Racecourse watched a Wrexham side dominate territory, possession and every underlying number that matters without once making a Villa goalkeeper work for a living. Then Yacel Amrizi found the finish the afternoon had been missing, and none of that mattered anymore.

"Dominant but no goal" was how the dressing room's own shorthand described the first hour, and it undersold, if anything, quite how one-sided a scoreless Premier League afternoon can be. Wrexham controlled the midfield from the opening whistle, forced Aston Villa into a back-foot afternoon they'll not want to relive on the coach home, and still found themselves level at the break against a Villa side that barely crossed the halfway line with intent. "We had the game, we just didn't have the goal," Jenkins said flatly at half-time — the kind of sentence a manager says a hundred times across a season and hopes never turns into a habit.

It didn't. Amrizi settled it in the 48th minute, ghosting off the last defender's shoulder and finishing with the low, unfussy composure that's become his trademark since the move to Wales — a goal that owed as much to patience as it did to quality, the product of a full hour of Wrexham probing for exactly the gap that finally opened. "We defended really well and controlled the midfield the whole match," Jenkins said afterward, more relaxed once the three points were secured than he'd sounded at the break. "Just took us a while to find the door. Some days you have to keep knocking." Amrizi, typically economical in the mixed zone, put it in fewer words still: "Wasn't pretty. Ball went in. That's the job."

It's also, increasingly, the kind of grinding, patient, unglamorous three points that separates a Premier League side building toward something durable from one riding a hot run of form — and with Liverpool away next up on the calendar, Jenkins will take a result built on control and stubbornness over one built on fireworks every time.

Controlled from start to finish, this one was memorable less for the scoreline than for who was standing in goal — a reminder that not every squad decision this window has actually taken effect yet.

The football itself was exactly what Keeyvon Jenkins ordered: controlled, unhurried, and largely stress-free. Jorthy Mokio opened the scoring from midfield, Joenathan Amelia doubled it and picked up Man of the Match honours for his trouble, and a front line rotated hard for cup duty — Vladyslav Veleten and Chido Obi both providing assists — did just enough to keep Preston's home crowd quiet without ever needing to find a third. Lewis O'Brien, restored to the middle of the park for a rare start, put in the kind of unfussy 6.8 that doesn't make headlines but keeps a manager's rotation policy looking justified.

Sandoval's Four Goals Cap a 10–0 Final as Wrexham's Kids Retain the Youth Rush Title

A second straight Rush final ended the same way as the first one did — as a procession — with a new German goalkeeper watching most of it from thirty yards back with nothing to do.

There is a rhythm to these finals now. Wrexham's academy walks in as favourites, the opposition's back four spends the first ten minutes finding out why, and by half-time Owen Meredith's notebook has stopped trying to track individual moves and started just counting goals. Arsenal's youth side arrived at the neutral venue on the back of a run that had, by all accounts, impressed people. It did not survive first contact with Fabricio Sandoval, who scored four times inside a game that finished 10–0 and, in doing so, produced the single best individual return of Wrexham's academy season so far.

Sandoval was not alone. Jules Collin added two more to a Rush campaign that has quietly turned him into the pipeline's most reliable finisher, Mathieu Drouet — fifteen years old as of this week and still finding his ceiling — scored once and set up two others, and Marco Soria orchestrated three assists from a wide role that Wrexham's coaching staff have been shaping him toward all year. Emiliano Bianchi, more often a holding presence than a scorer, got his name on the sheet too. In goal, Mika Horn — the German teenager signed into the academy earlier this month — collected a first meaningful appearance behind a defence that barely had to defend, a gentle introduction to life at Wrexham that his development staff will take gladly.

Wasteful Wrexham Made to Pay for Defensive Lapses as Manchester United Leave the Racecourse with All Three Points

Wrexham created more against Manchester United than they had in any match all season and have nothing to show for it beyond a losing scoreline — the kind of afternoon that flatters an opponent's ruthlessness far more than it exposes any real gulf in class.

The number that will needle Keeyvon Jenkins longest here isn't the scoreline, it's the expected-goals count: 2.9, by the club analytics staff's own reading, against a Manchester United side that scored twice from considerably less. Yacel Amrizi's 32nd-minute leveler — a low, driven finish that had briefly quieted the away end's early confidence — should have been the platform for a home win against a United team missing some of the swagger that's carried them to the top of the table this season. Instead, two defensive lapses either side of half-time turned a match Wrexham were controlling for long stretches into a first home Premier League defeat since Tottenham back in September. "We had the game," Jenkins said afterward, jaw tight, in a press conference stripped of his usual post-defeat evenness. "We had the ball, we had the moments, and we gave it away twice in ways we don't give it away in training. That's on us, not on how good they are."

United's manager, never one to undersell his own side's ruthlessness, was happy to frame it differently in the tunnel: "Good teams punish mistakes. We've been punishing mistakes all season. Today wasn't complicated." It's the kind of quote Owen Meredith's column has been building toward all autumn — the gap between a Wrexham side that can compete phase-for-phase with the league's best and a Wrexham side that still hasn't learned to close matches out against them. Callum Doyle, exposed for the second of the two United goals, was blunt about it walking off: "We know what happened. We'll watch it back and it won't be complicated to fix, but it needs fixing before it costs us again."

There were individual performances worth salvaging from the wreckage. Liberato Cacace's rating of 7.4 was the best of any Wrexham player on the pitch, a continuation of a autumn that has quietly made the Wide Half-turned-right-back one of the most reliable names in Jenkins's XI regardless of who's missing from midfield. Amrizi's goal was his second of the campaign and came at a moment the side badly needed it, even if it ultimately bought only a few minutes of parity rather than anything more durable. But the story of the day, inescapably, belonged to the two moments Wrexham conceded rather than the many more they created and didn't finish.

There is no Premier League fixture to report on yet — that starts in August — but the Youth Academy Rush Tournament has quietly become the most interesting thing happening at the training ground this summer, and Tuesday's 3–1 win over Watford was exhibit A. Soria opened it, Lord doubled it before half-time, and Juan Cruz Vargas, still just sixteen and fresh off being crowned Rising Star for a season he barely featured in, added the third with the unhurried confidence of a kid who already knows this level isn't the one that's going to test him.

It is easy, watching these academy fixtures, to forget how strange this club's summer has been. A month ago the first-team page had five more names on it. Now it doesn't, and nobody in the building seems especially torn up about it.

Every one of these lads has been told the same thing since January: the tournament isn't a consolation prize, it's an audition.

— Academy staff, speaking to the Dispatch on condition of anonymity

Whatever the audition is for, Vargas keeps passing it.

Five Out the Door, One Kid Sent Packing — and Somewhere in There, a Golden Goal

Jenkins clears the goalkeeping logjam and cashes in on last season's fringe players in the same afternoon Wrexham needed extra time to beat Ipswich's kids.

It was supposed to be a Youth Academy Rush Tournament fixture, the kind of thing that barely registers outside the training ground. Instead May 15th turned into the busiest day of Wrexham's summer so far. By the time Matthews' golden goal had settled a 2–1 extra-time win over Ipswich, the real news had already broken elsewhere: Danny Ward sold to Utrecht for $1.3m, Josh Windass to Wolves for $2.6m, Nathan Broadhead to Olympique Lyonnais for $4.5m, Zak Vyner to SC Braga for $2.4m, and Davis Keillor-Dunn to Levante for $2.55m — five first-team departures in a single afternoon, the biggest single-day clearout of the Jenkins era.

Ward's exit had been coming since he handed in a transfer request earlier in the summer, unhappy despite a completed development plan; sources close to the club say Jenkins was already leaning toward moving him on regardless, with Arthur Okonkwo returning from injury and seventeen-year-old Mason Webber next in line for minutes between the posts. The other four are harder to read as anything but a straightforward culling of the squad's fringes — useful last season, surplus to a Premier League one.

The academy had its own reckoning. Oliver Horne and Pieter Dijkgraaf both asked out, judged not yet ready for a first-team pathway and unwilling to wait. Anton Gassner didn't ask — he was shown the door, after deciding, apparently out loud and more than once, that he was already good enough for the senior squad. Wrexham's academy staff were unambiguous about the message that sends to the seventeen others still waiting their turn: no ultimatums. In his place, the club has brought in a new young goalkeeper, Lenny Kuhn, to restock the position.

You can want it. You can even be right that you want it. What you can't do is tell this club it owes you a shirt.

— A source inside the academy, on the Gassner decision

All of which made the actual football almost an afterthought. Lord and Matthews shared the goals, the second arriving deep into extra time to spare Wrexham's kids a penalty shootout. Nobody at the training ground seemed to be thinking about it by full time. There was too much else going on.
