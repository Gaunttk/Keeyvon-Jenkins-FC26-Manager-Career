The Board's Mandate

There is no version of a "survive and grow" mandate left to give — Wrexham won the league outright last May, in their first-ever Premier League season, and now carry a trophy into a campaign that will also be the club's first taste of continental football. The board's job-security read on Jenkins is unambiguous: Safe, five stars, no asterisks. Business so far has been almost entirely value-driven — a promising teenage striker plucked from outside the traditional scouting map rather than a marquee arrival — which tracks with a boardroom that built this rise on smart recruitment rather than outspending the division. One deal did get away: a move for Belgian free-agent CAM Yanis Callens fell through in the final stretch of negotiations when a rival club matched Wrexham's terms and beat it on wages. The board's response was to shrug and move on rather than chase the fee upward — which says as much about the recruitment philosophy here as any deal that actually closes.

"We had a deal agreed with Callens in our heads before it was agreed anywhere else. That happens. What matters is we didn't panic-spend to chase it once it slipped — we found a different kind of value instead."

— Keeyvon Jenkins, on losing out on Yanis Callens and signing Musa Tine

A mid-table target the summer after winning the league reads, on its face, as a step down in ambition. It isn't — it's an honest read of a schedule that now has three competitions in it instead of two, a squad that sent fifteen players out on loan for development, and a manager who has said plainly that he'd rather under-promise against a league that spent all summer studying how Wrexham beat them than pretend last season repeats itself by default. The Europa League target sets the real, longer horizon: not a one-year fluke defended and then lost, but a program that keeps climbing on its own terms. And before a single Premier League ball is kicked, there's a trophy already on the calendar to chase — the FA Community Shield, at Wembley, against the club that beat Wrexham to the Callens deal by outbidding on wages: Manchester City.

An invitation from the board that reads as much like a scouting trip as a tournament: three warm-up friendlies against Espanyol, Mallorca and a M'gladbach side rebuilding after relegation trouble of its own, all in Germany, all with the explicit brief to experiment with the lineup and tactics before a ball matters. There's a carrot attached — a first-place finish unlocks extra transfer budget from a board that has otherwise kept the purse closed all summer — but the real value is squad-wide minutes for a group that sent fifteen players out on loan and needs the rest to arrive at Community Shield week sharp, not just fit.

A first-ever Community Shield appearance for a club that spent fifteen seasons in non-league football inside living memory of half its academy intake's parents. Manchester City arrive as FA Cup holders and the deepest squad in the division on paper — a proper measuring-stick fixture, level one of a season that only gets harder from here, and the same club that quietly out-negotiated Wrexham for Yanis Callens this summer. Jenkins has been careful not to frame it as a grudge match in public. Privately, several players have used exactly that word.

Squad Breakdown — Champions, Thinner Than the Table Suggests

Forty names sit on the senior roster, but fifteen of them are out on loan this season — Amelia, Cadamarteri, Minda, Ortega, Cuenca, Soria, Lord, Macia, Webber, Kopp, Bianchi, Martini, Sandoval, Brunel and Faure, plus two academy graduates promoted and loaned out the same day, Bertrand and Salaun — which is the right call for most of their development, but it means the group actually training at the Racecourse is closer to twenty-five bodies. The spine that won the league is intact and, in most cases, still improving. The gaps are real, and they're concentrated at the two ends of the pitch: goalkeeping depth behind Okonkwo, left-back cover behind Doyle, and a striker department reduced to one fit senior No. 9 with Yacel Amrizi still working back from a torn quad.

Bottom line: this is not a squad that needs to spend big to compete on three fronts — it needs one more senior striker and a genuine left-back, ideally before either gap costs points in a season with no margin for a thin patch. The Tine business is the model going forward, same as it would have been with Callens: value over headlines, upside over marquee fees, and no panic when a target walks. That approach built a Premier League title out of a Championship squad in two seasons. There's no reason to abandon it now that the trophy's actually in the cabinet.

"Winning It Once Was the Easy Part" — Keeyvon Jenkins on Defending a Title He Still Can't Quite Believe

Owen Meredith sits down with the Head Coach three months removed from the trophy lift, ahead of Wrexham's first Champions League campaign.

Three months on from Leeds at the Racecourse, and Keeyvon Jenkins still hasn't put the trophy anywhere permanent. It sits, for now, in a glass case just outside the first-team dressing room at Colliers Park, close enough that every player walks past it on the way to training. "That's deliberate," he tells me, when I ask. "I want them looking at it every single day and thinking about how hard it was to get, not how easy it'll be to keep."

Owen Meredith: Let's start with the target itself. Mid-table, for a defending champion. That's a lower bar than plenty of fans expected to hear.

Keeyvon Jenkins: I know how that sounds. But I've watched this league punish teams that repeat themselves for a living. Every club we beat last year spent the summer studying exactly how we beat them. We're not the surprise anymore — we're the target. Mid-table is the honest read of that, not a lack of ambition.

OM: And yet you're also about to play your first-ever Champions League match. That's not a small thing to fold into a "mid-table" season.

KJ: No, it isn't — which is exactly why I've said the league phase is the target there, not further. Three competitions is a different kind of season physically and mentally than anything this group has done. I'd rather be honest about that now than pretend we're built to chase four trophies in year one of European football.

We didn't get here by spending like the big clubs. We got here by being smarter about where the money goes than anyone expected.

— Keeyvon Jenkins

OM: Let's talk about the summer's strangest week. You had a deal essentially agreed with Yanis Callens — free transfer, four-year contract, medical practically booked — and then it just wasn't there anymore.

KJ: [laughs] Football's football. We thought we had it done on our terms. Somebody came in over the top on wages at the last minute and Yanis took it — I don't blame him for a second, it's his career and his money. It stung for about a day. Then you move on, because the alternative is sulking about a deal that was never actually signed.

OM: And moving on turned into Musa Tine — seventeen years old, from Gabon, essentially unknown before this summer. That's about as far from Callens as a signing can get.

KJ: Completely different profile, completely different bet. Callens was the safe, proven piece. Tine is the opposite — no track record anyone can point to, discovered almost by accident, and we're betting on what our own eyes told us in two weeks of training rather than on a reputation. I'll be honest, some of our scouting staff thought I was mad. But you don't win a Championship-to-Premier-League title in two seasons by only doing the safe version of business.

OM: Your squad review this summer flagged the striker position as thin with Amrizi still out. Does a raw seventeen-year-old actually solve that?

KJ: Not on his own, no — and I won't pretend it does. Obi is still our senior No. 9 right now, and that's still a gap I'd like to close before the window shuts, transfer or patience with Yacel's recovery. Tine is a different kind of answer — eighteen months from now, not eighteen days. But you should see him finish a training-ground move. There's something real there.

OM: Last one. You told me last summer you never got to find out what you'd have been at this level as a player. A year on, with a Premier League medal, does that feeling change at all?

KJ: Some. Winning it answers a version of the question. But defending it is its own question, and I don't have the answer to that one yet — nobody at this club does. That's the whole job this season, if you want the honest version. Not the football. Whether we can stay hungry enough to deserve what's already in that glass case.

Training's called, and Jenkins is gone before I can ask a follow-up — same as last summer, same restlessness underneath the calm. Whatever this season turns out to be, it's clear he still isn't treating any of it as finished business.
