The Board's Mandate

A club statement released to the dressing room and, in redacted form, to the press: Wrexham's ownership group has framed Season 2 not as a lap of honour but as the first real test of the project's identity. The transfer budget has been more than doubled from last summer's operating room — a vote of confidence, and a floor being raised under Jenkins at the same time.

1. Avoid relegationNon-negotiable

Transfer budget$75,000,000

"Our movement at Wrexham is to become the Premiere Club in the Premiere League. We will accomplish this by developing a world-class youth academy and by identifying young up-and-comers who are willing to come in and work."

— Ownership statement, summer 2026

It is a patient statement dressed up as an urgent one. Survival first — everything else, including the academy pipeline the club has quietly begun building out of Germany, Brazil, and France, is a multi-season bet the board is explicitly asking Jenkins to make room for rather than sacrifice for a quick top-half finish.

New Arrival — The Assistant Manager's Chair
Earle Durow
Assistant Manager · Wrexham AFC

Durow, 46, spent eleven years in Bundesliga 2 and Bundesliga academy structures — six as a youth-development coordinator at Borussia Mönchengladbach, five running recruitment analysis for Union Berlin's B team — before Jenkins poached him in the close season on the strength of one conversation about pressing triggers that reportedly ran four hours long. He's never worked in English football before. He is, by his own admission, still getting used to being asked his opinion on penalty routines in a Welsh accent he can only half understand.

What he brings that Wrexham didn't have: a genuinely obsessive eye for age-16-to-21 recruitment data, and a scouting network he's rapidly expanding beyond Germany into Brazil and France — three markets he's convinced are underpriced relative to the talent coming through them. Germany has already produced a signing (see: Leo Bergmann); Brazil and France are still in the groundwork phase. He also brings a blunt, undiplomatic style in team meetings that Jenkins has publicly said he asked for. "I hired Earle to tell me when I'm wrong," Jenkins told the in-house media team. "He's already done it twice this week."

"I Didn't Take This Job to Survive It" — Keeyvon Jenkins on the Premier League, the Board, and What Comes Next

Owen Meredith sits down with the Head Coach ahead of Wrexham's first-ever top-flight season.

I meet Keeyvon Jenkins in the corner of the new Kop stand construction site, which is either a deliberate piece of stagecraft or the only quiet place left on the grounds this week. He's in training kit, coffee in hand, and visibly enjoying himself in a way I haven't seen from him since the trophy lift in May. We talk for the better part of forty minutes. Here's most of it.

Owen Meredith: Let's start with the obvious one. Eighteen months ago you're mid-table Championship, no guarantees. Now you're taking Wrexham into the Premier League for the first time in its history. Has it sunk in yet?

Keeyvon Jenkins: Honestly? No. And I don't want it to, not really — not before a ball's kicked. I've seen what happens to teams that show up to the top flight acting like they've already arrived. We haven't arrived at anything. We got promoted. That's a start line, not a finish line.

OM: The board's given you a budget more than double last summer's. Some fans are already talking about a top-half finish. The stated goal from ownership is survival first. Which one are you actually planning for?

KJ: Survival. Every single day. I appreciate the fans dreaming bigger — that's their job, not mine. My job is making sure we're still a Premier League club in May. If we do that and something extra happens on top of it, great. But I've watched too many good coaches get fired chasing a fan's timeline instead of a board's.

The academy isn't a nice-to-have anymore. It's the actual plan. Everything else is just how we survive long enough for it to work.

— Keeyvon Jenkins

OM: You've brought in Earle Durow from the German academy system specifically. That's a pretty pointed hire for a club that's supposedly focused on survival first.

KJ: It's not a contradiction, it's the whole point. Survival buys us time. The academy is what we're buying time for. Earle's already found players in Germany's lower divisions that bigger clubs haven't looked at twice, and we're pushing the same approach into Brazil and France now. That pipeline is going to matter more in three years than anything we do in the transfer market this window.

OM: There's still a version of this story where the American coach and the Hollywood owners are the whole narrative, whether you like it or not. Does that ever get old?

KJ: [laughs] It's not going anywhere, is it? Look, I get it. It's a good story. I'd probably write it too if I were you. What I'd ask is that people actually watch the team play before they decide what the story is. We press, we build from the back, we try to be brave with the ball. That's not an American import — that's just good football. I learned most of it in Denmark, if we're being precise about it.

OM: Last one, and it's a personal one. You've talked before about the injury that ended your playing career. Does a season like this — a first top-flight campaign, as a manager instead of a player — change how you think about that?

KJ: Every single day, if I'm honest with you. I never got to find out what I would've been at this level as a player. I get a version of that answer now, just from the touchline instead of the pitch. It's not the same thing. But it's not nothing, either. My kids get to watch me find out. That matters to me more than it probably should.

We wrap there — training's starting, and Jenkins is already looking past me toward the pitch. Whatever the Premier League turns out to be for this club, it's clear he's not treating it as a victory lap. Whether that's the right instinct, or just the only one he's got, is a question this pressroom will be asking all season.
